# Interview Analysis — NHS Mental Health Practitioner

> Part of the DRP 2026 qualitative research corpus. Analysed against the locked
> problem scope: **Option C — UK adults in the ADHD diagnostic queue and the first
> 12 months post-diagnosis.**
> This respondent is a system-side practitioner, not a patient. The analysis
> framework is adapted accordingly: emphasis is on system-side evidence, practitioner
> experience, and design implications the practitioner's vantage point uniquely
> unlocks.

---

## 1. Respondent Profile

**Role:** NHS Mental Health Practitioner  
**Experience band:** 1–5 years  
**Survey submitted:** 20 May 2026  
**Vantage point:** Frontline clinical practice within NHS mental health services;
direct contact with ADHD patients and the wider mental health system  
**Significance to the research corpus:** One of two NHS practitioner respondents
(the other being the Nurse, 6–10 years experience). Together they triangulate
patient-side lived experience with system-side clinical observation. This MHP
respondent provides the clearest articulation of downstream social harms
(self-medication, antisocial behaviour, police contact, A&E displacement) and
the only demand-side critique (broadening diagnostic criteria adding referral
pressure) in the entire corpus.

## 2. Key Verbatim Quotes

Quotes are reproduced exactly as written. Thematic groupings reflect the
analytical framework; the survey questions are included in each entry to preserve
context.

---

### Theme A: Wait Times

> "Delays are in assessment waiting and then in starting treatment; around 3 years
> for an adult patient."

*Evidences: a specific, system-internal estimate of 3 years end-to-end (assessment
wait plus treatment initiation), confirming and slightly extending the Nurse's
"3–5 years" figure. Crucially, the MHP frames the delay as two-stage — assessment
wait and then treatment commencement — meaning even reaching assessment does not
mark the end of the void.*

---

### Theme B: Patient Consequences

> "Difficult to function optimally due to the impact on functioning; some
> self-medicate with substances."

*Evidences: self-medication with substances as an observed, named clinical
consequence of untreated ADHD during the wait — the only explicit mention of
substance use as a coping pathway in the entire practitioner corpus. This is a
harm-reduction signal with direct design implications.*

> "Impact on mental health from decreased functionality, plus effects on education
> or work due to poor attention and poor coping."

*Evidences: the wait degrades functioning across multiple life domains simultaneously
(mental health, education, employment) — corroborating patient-reported experiences
(Daniel, 29yr) from the clinical side.*

> "Emotional difficulties associated with ADHD."

*Evidences: patients most commonly misunderstand the emotional regulation dimension
of ADHD (emotional dysregulation, rejection sensitivity) rather than the
attention-and-hyperactivity surface presentation. This is a content gap the
touchpoint could address.*

---

### Theme C: System Pressures

> "Increased burden on mental health services — cases that could be managed with
> treatment go unmanaged, leading to more presentations at A&E and emergency
> services."

*Evidences: the A&E displacement pathway — untreated ADHD-related deterioration
routing into emergency services, which is a measurable system-level cost that
could anchor cost-effectiveness arguments in stakeholder messaging.*

> "Workload is heavily focused on acute case management, limiting the ability to
> provide longer-term support to those who also need help."

*Evidences: NHS capacity is structurally captured by acute triage, making
sub-acute or ongoing low-intensity ADHD support invisible within the system's
current operating logic. This is the mechanism behind Insight 2 ("Diagnosis is a
one-time event, not a service").*

> "A burden across all services, as priorities are not clearly defined. Individuals
> who could significantly benefit from timely intervention may not receive the
> help they need."

*Evidences: a systemic prognosis of deterioration absent structural change;
describes diffuse burden rather than a single-service bottleneck, suggesting
the cost of inaction propagates across the whole health and social system.*

---

### Theme D: Practitioner Experience

> "You recognise someone needs help but are unable to provide it."

*Evidences: moral distress expressed with unusual economy — this is the most
emotionally concentrated statement in the practitioner corpus. It names
professional helplessness without deflection, and implicitly positions a
non-clinical touchpoint as something that could partially resolve the practitioner's
distress by giving patients something to be signposted toward.*

> "Yes, we do."

*Evidences: collective (team-level) acknowledgement of being overstretched,
consistent with the Nurse's parallel confirmation; establishes systemic rather than
individual burnout.*

---

### Theme E: Demand-Side Critique

> "The diagnostic criteria are broad, leading to more referrals of people with
> relatively mild attention difficulties, adding further pressure on services."

*Evidences: the only explicit demand-side critique in the corpus. Broadening criteria
increases referral volume at the mild end, compressing available capacity for those
with significant impairment. This introduces a severity-spectrum challenge: the
diagnostic queue contains both mild and severe cases, and any platform serving
"people in the queue" will need to accommodate that range.*

---

### Theme F: Deprioritisation of the Less Vocal

> "Individuals who are less vocal about their needs are often overlooked despite
> requiring support. Many with mental health difficulties struggle to come forward
> even in significant distress; when they do, overburdened services and the
> prioritising of more acute or visible cases can obscure the full picture of need."

*Evidences: a double-layer exclusion mechanism — first, difficulty self-advocating
keeps people from presenting at all; second, when they do present, system triage
filters them out in favour of more acute or behaviourally visible cases. Directly
implies a design imperative: the touchpoint must be accessible to people who are
not yet able to advocate loudly for themselves.*

---

### Theme G: Criminal-Justice Pathway

> "Yes. People who lack proper routine (work or education) and are using substances
> are at high risk of antisocial behaviour and police involvement."

*Evidences: the criminal-justice pathway as a sequenced, multi-step progression
(unstructured time + substance use → antisocial behaviour → police involvement).
This is the only mention of police contact in the corpus and marks the most acute
downstream harm articulated by any respondent. It is clinically observed rather
than speculated, which gives it particular weight as an impact framing.*

## 3. Thematic Analysis

### Theme 1: The Self-Medication and Substance Use Pathway

The most clinically significant consequence named by this practitioner is that some
patients "self-medicate with substances" during the wait. This is not a background
detail — it is a direct response to the absence of clinical support. The causal
chain is observable: untreated symptoms → impaired functioning → substance use as
a coping mechanism. Compounded by the criminal-justice pathway observation (substance
use as a precursor to antisocial behaviour and police involvement), this creates a
bifurcating trajectory for the most vulnerable patients: those with weak routine
and support structures slide toward substance use and legal consequences, while
those with stronger scaffolding (employment, education, family) tend to hold but
still deteriorate across mental health, work, and relationships.

This pathway is entirely absent from NHS support during the wait, and partly absent
from the patient interview corpus (participants were generally higher-functioning).
It represents the population the team will reach *less* easily through standard
recruitment, but who arguably stand to benefit *most* from early structured support.

### Theme 2: The A&E Displacement Effect

The MHP describes a predictable system dynamic: cases "that could be managed with
treatment go unmanaged, leading to more presentations at A&E and emergency
services." This is not a vague claim about future burden — it describes a current
observable pattern in which preventable deterioration is redirecting patients into
crisis-response infrastructure. The cost-effectiveness implication is precise:
A&E and emergency service contacts are among the most expensive NHS touchpoints;
a non-clinical intervention that delays or prevents even a fraction of these
presentations has a calculable system value. This is the strongest available
argument for the touchpoint's cost-effectiveness, and should be a primary
stakeholder-messaging frame.

### Theme 3: Moral Distress and the Practitioner as Latent Ally

"You recognise someone needs help but are unable to provide it" is the most
affectively concentrated statement in the corpus and uniquely practitioners can
make it: only someone who has clinical knowledge of what a patient needs and no
capacity to deliver it can experience this specific form of distress. This matters
for design not as a curiosity but as a stakeholder signal: practitioners in moral
distress are actively looking for anything they can signpost patients toward. They
are not passive; they are constrained. A touchpoint that is credible enough for
clinical signposting becomes a partial resolution to the practitioner's distress,
not just a patient-facing tool. This opens a secondary user journey the team may
not yet have modelled: the practitioner as a referral node who actively sends
patients to the touchpoint during clinical contact.

### Theme 4: Deprioritisation and the Double Exclusion of the Less Vocal

The MHP identifies a two-stage exclusion mechanism operating against patients who
struggle to self-advocate. Stage one: many with mental health difficulties cannot
come forward at all, even in significant distress (barrier to presentation). Stage
two: when they do reach services, overburdened triage and the visibility of more
acute cases means their needs are not fully captured (barrier to recognition). The
patients most likely to fall through both stages are those who are quieter,
less behaviourally disruptive, and less articulate about their distress — not
necessarily those with milder impairment. This creates a systematic gap between
clinical need and clinical reach that the touchpoint must address through design:
low-friction entry, anonymity options, and no requirement to self-label or
self-diagnose to access support.

### Theme 5: The Demand-Side Critique — A Severity Spectrum in the Queue

The MHP's closing observation — that broad diagnostic criteria increase referrals
of people with "relatively mild attention difficulties" — is the only demand-side
critique in the entire corpus and introduces a genuine design tension. The diagnostic
queue is not a homogenous population: it spans people with significant functional
impairment who are deteriorating without support, and people with mild attention
difficulties whose clinical need is real but lower-acuity. Any platform serving
"people in the queue" is implicitly serving both ends of this spectrum. This means
the touchpoint cannot be calibrated exclusively for crisis-adjacent users (too
intensive for milder cases) or exclusively for the worried-well (insufficient for
severely impaired users). The design must accommodate a severity gradient, likely
through personalisation, user-driven depth of engagement, or explicit
sub-pathway design.

### Theme 6: Acute Case Management as a Structural Trap

The MHP describes an NHS currently "heavily focused on acute case management,
limiting the ability to provide longer-term support to those who also need help."
This is the mechanism behind the post-diagnosis cliff (Insight 2) and also explains
why sub-acute ADHD support never gets funded or staffed: the system's operating
logic rewards responsiveness to acute crisis, not sustained low-intensity
scaffolding. The incentive structure ensures that even if clinicians wanted to
provide longer-term support, the workload composition prevents it. This is
structurally stable: absent a deliberate policy reorientation, the gap will
persist. The implication for the touchpoint is that it is not filling a temporary
gap — it is filling a structurally permanent one. The value proposition should
be framed accordingly: not "bridging while the NHS catches up," but "providing
the layer the NHS is not designed to provide."

## 4. Distinctive Contribution

This MHP's contribution differs from all other respondents in the corpus in six
specific respects. No other respondent provides all six; several provide none.

### (a) The Self-Medication Pathway
"Some self-medicate with substances" — a clinically observed consequence of
untreated ADHD during the wait, naming a harm-reduction dimension the patient
interviews do not surface. The practitioner can see this population; the team's
recruiting methods are unlikely to reach them directly.

### (b) The Criminal-Justice Pathway
"High risk of antisocial behaviour and police involvement" — the most severe
downstream consequence named in the corpus, observed to follow a specific sequence
(no routine + substance use → antisocial behaviour → police). This is the clearest
articulation of the social harms of the diagnostic wait available in the research
corpus.

### (c) The A&E Displacement Effect
"More presentations at A&E and emergency services" — a system-level consequence
directly quantifiable in NHS cost terms. No other respondent names A&E as a
downstream destination for untreated ADHD cases. This is the strongest available
lever for cost-effectiveness stakeholder messaging.

### (d) Moral Distress
"You recognise someone needs help but are unable to provide it" — the most
emotionally direct statement in the entire corpus. Uniquely situated: only a
practitioner with clinical knowledge and insufficient capacity can experience
this precise form of helplessness. This statement also opens the practitioner
as a potential referral node — an underexplored secondary stakeholder role.

### (e) The Demand-Side Counter-Current
"The diagnostic criteria are broad, leading to more referrals of people with
relatively mild attention difficulties" — the only voice in the corpus to name
the supply-demand mismatch from the supply side. This is not a criticism of
patients but an observation about system design. It introduces the severity-spectrum
challenge the team must address.

### (f) The Deprioritisation of the Less Vocal
"Individuals who are less vocal about their needs are often overlooked despite
requiring support" — a named mechanism of systematic exclusion operating within
the NHS that is distinct from mere capacity shortage. It identifies *which*
patients are lost even when they do present. This is the sharpest design brief
for accessible, low-barrier onboarding in the corpus.

## 5. Connection to Locked Problem Scope

The locked scope (Option C) focuses on "UK adults in the ADHD diagnostic queue
and the first 12 months post-diagnosis." This MHP's response validates Option C
with stronger system-side specificity than any other source in the corpus:

**Validation of the diagnostic wait as the primary intervention window.** The MHP
confirms a 3-year wait as a clinical estimate, identifies specific deterioration
pathways during that wait (substance self-medication, criminal-justice, A&E
presentation), and observes that cases "that could be managed with treatment go
unmanaged." The wait is not empty by design — it is empty by default, because no
layer of the system has been tasked with occupying it.

**Validation of the post-diagnosis cliff.** The MHP's observation that workload
is "heavily focused on acute case management, limiting the ability to provide
longer-term support" directly explains why the first 12 months post-diagnosis
are also largely unsupported. Diagnosis does not unlock sustained clinical
attention; it concludes the acute phase and ejects the patient back into
unsupported daily life.

**The challenging piece: a severity spectrum.** The demand-side critique
(broadening criteria → milder cases in the queue) challenges the team to
interrogate whether the touchpoint is designed for a single severity level or
for a range. The locked problem statement ("UK adults in the ADHD diagnostic
queue") implicitly includes mild-to-severe. If the touchpoint is calibrated
only for significant impairment, it may not serve the growing number of
milder-difficulty users in the queue; if calibrated only for milder cases,
it may be inadequate for those most at risk of self-medication and criminal-justice
pathways. The design must accommodate this spectrum — likely through user-directed
depth of engagement — or must explicitly articulate which part of the spectrum
it targets.

**Named system pathways not in the Nurse's response.** The Nurse focused on
operational strain, waiting list administration, and broad clinical consequences.
This MHP names specific social-system pathways (self-medication, antisocial
behaviour, police, A&E) that extend the team's harm narrative from "people
struggle during the wait" to "people enter downstream crisis systems during
the wait." This is materially stronger as a pitch-impact frame.

## 6. Stakeholder Signals

**Acute moral distress.** "You recognise someone needs help but are unable to
provide it" is not a bureaucratic complaint — it is an expression of professional
helplessness. A practitioner in this state is not defending the status quo; they
are actively seeking alternatives. This signal should inform how the team frames
the touchpoint to practitioner stakeholders: not as a replacement for clinical
care, but as something clinicians can hand patients during (and after) clinical
contact. The phrasing "a referral or signposting destination" in the design
implications (see §8) directly responds to this signal.

**Explicit support-layer gap.** The MHP identifies that the NHS cannot provide
"longer-term support to those who also need help" under current workload conditions.
This is not a feature request for better NHS services; it is a practitioner
acknowledging a structural gap and implicitly endorsing the existence of a
non-clinical support layer. This is the clearest professional endorsement in the
corpus for a digital touchpoint of the type the team is designing.

**Deprioritisation as a systemic (not individual) failure.** The MHP does not
attribute patient invisibility to clinician negligence or poor motivation; they
attribute it to overburdened services and triage logic that rewards visible acuity.
This framing protects the practitioner-as-ally narrative: clinicians are also
victims of the system's operating logic. A touchpoint designed to reach the less
vocal is not competing with clinical care — it is complementing a system that,
by design, cannot reach them.

The following quotes and observations are recommended for the M1 elevator pitch.
Each is short enough to cite verbatim in a 3-minute presentation and strong enough

**1. For moral framing / emotional resonance:**

Use to illustrate the human cost to practitioners and to establish that this is a

**2. For impact framing / downstream harm:**

Use to extend the harm narrative beyond "people struggle at work" to the most

**3. For cost-effectiveness / system stakeholder messaging:**
> "Cases that could be managed with treatment go unmanaged, leading to more

Use to make a cost-effectiveness argument to NHS or policy stakeholders: preventing

Use to explain why low-friction, accessible design is not a nice-to-have but a

the NHS systematically fails to reach.

## 8. Design Implications

### (a) Design for the Less Vocal: Low-Friction, Anonymous Onboarding
The MHP explicitly names the less vocal as a population systematically overlooked
by services. This is a design brief: the touchpoint must not require confident
self-advocacy to access. Practical implications: anonymous browsing before
account creation; no requirement to self-label as "ADHD" or "diagnosed" to
enter; low-cognitive-load onboarding that does not front-load forms or
self-assessment. Users who cannot articulate their needs should still be able
to benefit from the platform within the first session.

### (b) Harm-Reduction Awareness: Some Users Will Be Self-Medicating
The MHP's observation that "some self-medicate with substances" is a content
warning for the design team. A non-trivial fraction of the in-queue population
will be using alcohol or drugs as coping mechanisms. The touchpoint's content
and peer-community dynamics should be informed by harm-reduction principles:
non-judgmental framing of coping behaviours, access to relevant support resources,
and care not to create content that inadvertently stigmatises or alienates users
who are self-medicating. This does not require the touchpoint to become a
harm-reduction service — but it should not assume a population uniformly free of
substance use.

### (c) Accommodate a Severity Spectrum
The demand-side critique (broad criteria → mild cases in the queue alongside
severely impaired cases) means the platform cannot be single-calibrated. Design
options: user-directed depth of engagement (users choose how deeply to engage
with structured support), multiple entry points keyed to different self-assessed
levels of difficulty, or a single flexible structure that is lightweight enough
not to overwhelm mild-difficulty users and substantive enough to genuinely help
severely impaired ones. The team should decide which approach is most consistent
with the locked HMW and the touchpoint's technical constraints.

### (d) Cost-Effectiveness vs A&E as the Core Stakeholder Value Proposition
For NHS commissioners, policy stakeholders, or project sponsors, the clearest
value proposition is cost avoidance: a non-clinical touchpoint that reduces the
rate at which unmanaged ADHD routes into A&E and emergency services. This should
be framed explicitly in stakeholder communications (pitch leaflet, HCD portfolio)
with reference to the MHP's observation. It is more compelling than a welfare
argument alone because it speaks to a system incentive (budget) rather than
a moral one.

### (e) Practitioners as Referral Nodes: A Secondary User Journey
The MHP's moral distress signal — wanting to give patients something but having
nothing to offer — points to an underexplored secondary user journey: the
practitioner as a referral or signposting actor. If the touchpoint is designed
to be credible enough for clinical signposting, practitioners become a distribution
channel that reaches patients at precisely the moment of clinical contact (when
they are most motivated and most in need). This would require the touchpoint to
have a practitioner-facing "about this tool" or "how to signpost" layer — a minimal
design addition with potentially significant reach implications. The team should
assess whether this is within scope for M2/M3 or a stretch goal for M4.
