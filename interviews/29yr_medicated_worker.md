# Interview Analysis — 29-Year-Old Medicated Worker

> Part of the DRP 2026 qualitative research corpus. Analysed against the locked
> problem scope: **Option C — UK adults in the ADHD diagnostic queue and the first
> 12 months post-diagnosis.**

---

## 1. Respondent Profile

**Age:** 29  
**Employment status:** Working professional (specific sector not stated)  
**Diagnosis status:** Formally diagnosed, medicated (stimulant medication implied by references to comedown and medication-off evenings)  
**Route to diagnosis:** Self-identified after struggling at work; GP referral followed by a private formal diagnosis (approximately 6 months, 2020); NHS wait from referral was estimated at over two years  
**Presentation (analyst inference):** Reports symptoms as "not particularly hyperactive," requiring deeper investigation to recognise themselves in ADHD criteria — consistent with an inattentive presentation, though the respondent does not self-classify  
**Key personal context:** Partner was instrumental in flagging behaviours; family conversations followed. Has lived with undiagnosed ADHD through at least early adult working life. Ongoing clinical support post-diagnosis has been minimal. Medication partially effective — does not resolve initiation difficulties, sleep disruption, or the structural absence of external demands at weekends. Has not yet accessed CBT or ADHD coaching but is interested. Open-office working environment remains actively problematic.

## 2. Key Verbatim Quotes

### Theme A — Systemic failure and opacity of the diagnostic process

> "NHS wait from referral was over two years. Process felt opaque — no sense of queue position."

*Evidences that the problem is not merely delay but the psychological harm of unacknowledged, invisible waiting — patients cannot plan, adapt, or seek alternatives if they cannot see where they stand.*

> "Essentially nothing between referral and assessment. A couple of leaflets, nothing practically useful."

*Evidences the 3-year void directly: the NHS offers process bureaucracy (leaflets) while providing zero substantive support during the highest-need pre-diagnostic window.*

> "Had to piece together what ADHD meant for their life entirely alone."

*Evidences the emotional and cognitive burden placed on the individual during the wait — framing the gap not as inconvenient but as a solo navigation task for a brain that struggles with exactly that kind of unstructured self-directed work.*

---

### Theme B — Peer community as the only working support layer

> "Online communities — practical real-world advice from people who live with ADHD. Peers with ADHD specifically — an immediacy of understanding that professionals rarely match."

*Evidences both the effectiveness of peer support and its qualitative superiority over clinical contact: the word "immediacy" points to something beyond information — it is affective recognition that professional encounters typically lack.*

> "Structured, community-based resource — not just forums, but guided support. Peer mentoring from further-along individuals. Practical help bridging diagnosis and functional change. Somewhere to ask everyday questions without wasting a professional's time."

*The single richest quote in the dataset for the project's HMW: this respondent has already articulated the exact service concept the team is designing toward, entirely unprompted. "Not just forums" draws the line between what exists and what is needed.*

---

### Theme C — Failure of generic productivity tools and the guilt mechanism

> "Habit tracking apps — felt like another thing to fail at, added guilt."

*Evidences the active harm done by neurotypical-productivity tooling: the failure is not neutral, it compounds shame. This is the mechanism that makes "build a better to-do app" the wrong design direction.*

> "Apps (Todoist, Notion) — useful in theory, engagement fades with novelty."

*Evidences ADHD's novelty-seeking dopamine dynamic: tools that rely on sustained intrinsic motivation are structurally incompatible with the ADHD brain, regardless of how well-designed they are.*

---

### Theme D — What medication does and does not solve

> "Initiation improved but not solved. Unstructured time (weekends) — medication alone does not create structure without external demands."

*Evidences that medication is a partial, context-dependent intervention — it attenuates symptoms but cannot substitute for external scaffolding. Directly challenges the implicit clinical assumption that prescribing closes the support loop.*

> "Sleep still disrupted; comedown can make evenings harder."

*Evidences a daily cycle where the medication-managed hours are productive but the bookending periods (morning wake-up, evening comedown) remain unmanaged — pointing to a need for support that wraps around medication, not just alongside it.*

---

### Theme E — Institutional inadequacy and the reactive-not-proactive failure mode

> "No. NHS waits leave people undiagnosed and unsupported for years. Real gap between what is supposed to exist and what is accessible. Support is reactive and one-size-fits-all rather than tailored or proactive."

*Evidences that this respondent has reached a precise systemic critique, not just personal frustration — the words "reactive," "one-size-fits-all," and "proactive" signal design literacy that makes their wishlist statements especially credible.*

## 3. Thematic Analysis

### Theme 1 — The opaque, empty wait

This respondent provides the clearest first-person account of what the diagnostic wait *feels like* from the inside. The word "opaque" is significant: the problem is not only that the wait is long, but that it is invisible — patients cannot calibrate, adapt, or plan because they have no queue position, no milestone, and no contact. The consequence of that opacity is described precisely: "had to piece together what ADHD meant for their life entirely alone." For a brain that struggles with unstructured, self-directed tasks — which is the defining feature of ADHD — being asked to self-navigate an unstructured multi-year wait is doubly cruel. The couple of leaflets offered represent the gap between bureaucratic acknowledgement and substantive support.

### Theme 2 — Peer community as the operational system

Across multiple questions, this respondent returns to online communities (specifically Reddit's r/ADHD) as the primary locus of practically useful support. Crucially, they distinguish between *information* (available everywhere) and *immediacy of understanding* (available only from people who live with ADHD). The quote "an immediacy of understanding that professionals rarely match" is not a complaint about professional competence — it identifies a qualitatively different form of support that clinical encounters structurally cannot deliver. This respondent also explicitly names the missing layer: "not just forums, but guided support" — acknowledging that while peer community is the effective layer, its current unstructured form is inadequate.

### Theme 3 — The guilt mechanism of neurotypical tools

This respondent used Todoist, Notion, physical planners, and habit-tracking apps — a representative sweep of mainstream productivity tooling — and offers granular failure modes for each. Habit trackers specifically "felt like another thing to fail at, added guilt." This is not vague dissatisfaction; it identifies a *mechanism*: tools built around consistency and streak maintenance are punishing for brains that struggle with exactly that. Todoist and Notion "fades with novelty" — a different mechanism, pointing to how dopamine-dependent the ADHD brain's engagement with digital tools is. Physical planners worked "but only once the habit was established" — indicating that the tools are not inherently broken, but that they cannot bootstrap the habits they depend on without external support.

### Theme 4 — Medication as partial, context-dependent scaffolding

This respondent is one of the few in the corpus who speaks with granularity about what medication does and does not address. The formulation "initiation improved but not solved" is clinically precise. "Unstructured time (weekends) — medication alone does not create structure without external demands" directly challenges the dominant clinical assumption that prescription closes the loop. The evening comedown is named as a specific unmanaged period. The practical implication is that support needs to wrap around the medication schedule, operating during the evening wind-down and weekend structure-free periods that medication cannot touch.

### Theme 5 — Articulate systemic critique as a design-literate respondent

More than any other participant, this respondent has synthesised their lived experience into a structural critique: "reactive and one-size-fits-all rather than tailored or proactive." This is not lay frustration — it is a systems-design observation. The wish-list they generate ("structured, community-based resource," "peer mentoring from further-along individuals," "somewhere to ask everyday questions without wasting a professional's time," "tools built around how ADHD brains actually work") maps almost exactly onto the service concept the team's HMW is pointing toward. The unprompted alignment between participant desire and team direction is strong pitch evidence.

## 4. Distinctive Contribution

This respondent's unique contribution is threefold and interrelated.

**First**, they are the only participant who combines diagnosis (post-diagnostic, medicated) with active working life in an open-office environment — making them the clearest proxy for the "worker with ADHD trying to function professionally" persona that no other interviewee embodies as directly. Their observations about open offices, deadline-driven motivation cycles, and the evening medication comedown are specific to this life context.

**Second**, they provide the most granular and mechanistically articulate account of tool failure in the corpus. Rather than saying productivity apps "don't work," they identify *why* — novelty decay, guilt compounding, habit-bootstrapping failure — in ways that translate directly into design constraints.

**Third**, they have independently arrived at almost exactly the same service concept as the team's HMW, using the same vocabulary ("structured," "guided," "peer mentoring," "bridging diagnosis and functional change"). This makes them the closest thing the team has to unprompted validation of the design direction from inside the target population.

This respondent sits at the intersection of both named stakeholder groups in Option C. They experienced the diagnostic queue (NHS wait of over two years, private route of 6 months) and are now in a post-diagnosis period where ongoing clinical support has been minimal. Whether they are still within "the first 12 months post-diagnosis" is not specified, but their account of the post-diagnostic cliff — "psychiatrist helpful at diagnosis but ongoing clinical support has been minimal" — is precisely the experience the scope is designed to address.

**Supports the problem statement directly.** The locked problem statement reads: "clinical support is delayed by years and ends abruptly after medication, while informal forums are unstructured and generic productivity apps add guilt rather than help, which leads them to rely on luck — whether they happen to find the right peer community — to bridge between diagnosis and functional change." Every clause of this statement is independently evidenced by this respondent's interview.

**Does not challenge or destabilise the scope.** There is no data from this respondent that would push back against Option C. Their out-of-scope needs (workplace reasonable adjustments, longer-term medication management) are not foregrounded; they remain within the transitional support gap that Option C targets.

## 8. Design Implications

### Implication 1 — Queue transparency as a minimum viable feature

The "no sense of queue position" observation identifies an unmet need that does not require clinical expertise to address: a simple mechanism for making the diagnostic journey visible (where am I, what comes next, what can I do now) would reduce the psychological burden of the wait significantly. **Failed tool:** the NHS referral process, by design, provides no position or milestone feedback.

*Tied to:* "Process felt opaque — no sense of queue position."

### Implication 2 — Do not build another to-do app; build accountability and co-presence

Engagement fades with novelty (Todoist, Notion) and consistency-based mechanics add guilt (habit trackers). The mechanism that *does* work — body-doubling, shared timers, peer accountability — is absent from all tools this respondent tried. The digital touchpoint must centre co-presence and external accountability rather than personal tracking. **Failed tools:** Todoist, Notion, habit-tracking apps — all broke on the novelty-decay or guilt-compounding axis.

*Tied to:* "Habit tracking apps — felt like another thing to fail at, added guilt" and "engagement fades with novelty."

### Implication 3 — Design for the medication-off hours, not just the productive window

The medication comedown (evenings) and unstructured periods (weekends) are the two largest unmanaged gaps in this user's daily cycle. A touchpoint that only operates during high-functioning daytime hours misses the moments of highest risk of routine collapse. Consider features specifically serving wind-down, next-day preparation, and weekend structure scaffolding. **Failed tool:** medication itself — effective during demand-structured hours, insufficient when external structure disappears.

*Tied to:* "Unstructured time (weekends) — medication alone does not create structure without external demands" and "comedown can make evenings harder."

### Implication 4 — Peer mentoring, not open forum — structure is the differentiating feature

This respondent explicitly distinguishes between existing forums ("not just forums") and what they actually need ("guided support," "peer mentoring from further-along individuals"). The design must impose *structure* on the peer layer — matched mentoring, guided check-ins, defined roles — rather than replicating the open-ended Reddit dynamic that is already available and already insufficient. **Failed tool:** Reddit r/ADHD — effective for information but structurally incapable of delivering sustained, personalised peer scaffolding.

*Tied to:* "Structured, community-based resource — not just forums, but guided support."

### Implication 5 — Friction reduction must be designed in, not left to habit formation

This respondent found that physical planners worked "only once the habit was established" — i.e., the tool depends on the very behaviour it is supposed to build. Every onboarding and daily interaction in the touchpoint must assume the user cannot reliably return on intrinsic motivation alone. Design must include low-friction entry points, contextual nudges from peers, and short interaction loops. Any feature that requires an established habit before it becomes useful will fail this user group before it starts. **Failed tool:** physical planners at habit-formation stage; Notion/Todoist for the same reason at digital scale.

*Tied to:* "Physical planners — worked better than digital but only once the habit was established" and "reducing friction — laying things out the night before, batching admin."
