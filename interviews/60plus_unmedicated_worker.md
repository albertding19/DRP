# Interview Analysis — 60+ Unmedicated Worker

> DRP 2026 | Imperial DoC × RCA | Option C locked scope
> Analyst: Claude Code (claude-sonnet-4-6) | Date: 2026-05-20

---

## 1. Respondent Profile

**Who they are.** An adult aged 60 or over, in full-time or part-time employment, who has *just* received an ADHD diagnosis after a journey spanning more than a year. They are currently unmedicated and awaiting medication — placing them at the very beginning of the post-diagnostic window the team has scoped to.

**Diagnosis route.** Their path was not self-originated; it was *led by a brother's diagnosis*, which prompted personal research online. From there: online symptom test → GP referral → NHS waiting list → roughly one year of silence → proactive phone call to discover the wait in their area was approximately **seven years** → independent research into the NHS Right to Choose (R2C) pathway → referral to an NHS-approved private clinic (March 2025) → diagnosis received → now waiting for medication. Total elapsed time: over one year to reach diagnosis. The seven-year figure represents the counterfactual had they not self-navigated to R2C.

**Medication status.** Unmedicated. Awaiting first prescription after recent diagnosis.

**Key personal context.**
- Workplace imposes a practical barrier to digital tools: employers may not tolerate visible phone use, making app-based interventions inaccessible during working hours.
- No formal support received at any stage — not during the wait, not at diagnosis.
- Primary coping strategies were developed through personal trial and error, not clinical guidance or peer community.
- Aware of ADHD-specific media content (YouTube) but has not yet used any dedicated apps.
- Expresses clear preferences for *specific* social/collaborative tool formats when asked, suggesting latent readiness to engage with the right product.

## 2. Key Verbatim Quotes

### Theme A — The system's failure to act

> **"7 years is a joke."**

*Evidences visceral, unambiguous system critique. The compression of a year of waiting, a phone call, and an absurd prognosis into four words is pitch-ready testimony to the scale of the diagnostic void — and to the emotional register in which older adults who have managed undiagnosed ADHD for decades receive this information.*

> **"[Online test → GP → NHS waiting list → no contact for a year → called to find out wait was ~7 years for their area → researched right-to-choose → NHS-approved private clinic → referral March 2025 → just received diagnosis → now awaiting medication. Took over a year.]"**

*Evidences that successful navigation of the system was entirely self-driven: each step forward was initiated by the respondent, not by the system. The NHS made no proactive contact. This is a textbook demonstration of Insight 1 ("the 3-year void") and Insight 2 ("diagnosis is a one-time event, not a service").*

> **"No [support during the waiting period]."**

*Evidences the starkness of the void — single-word confirmation that the NHS offered nothing between referral and diagnosis. The brevity of the answer mirrors the emptiness of the experience.*

### Theme B — Workplace and everyday disruption

> **"Most disrupted at work — requires organisation and completing tasks in sequence."**

*Evidences that the workplace is the primary arena of functional difficulty. The framing — "organisation" and "tasks in sequence" — maps directly onto working-memory and executive-function deficits that ADHD literature identifies as the central impairment in professional settings. This respondent is living the problem every working day, unmedicated, and with no formal support.*

> **"Difficulty completing started tasks. Multitasking is hard. Motivation can be difficult."**

*Evidences classic ADHD executive-function symptom clustering (task initiation / completion, attentional switching, motivation regulation) reported from the vantage point of someone who has managed these for 60+ years without knowing their cause. The measured, matter-of-fact tone is itself significant — this is not someone newly alarmed by symptoms but someone describing a lifelong baseline.*

### Theme C — Self-built coping, digital barriers

> **"Making lists and crossing off tasks (satisfying). Doing more in general. Exercise helps a lot."**

*Evidences decades of autonomous coping strategy development through trial and error. The word "satisfying" (re: crossing off tasks) reveals that this respondent has intuitively discovered the dopamine-reward loop that underlies effective ADHD task management — without any clinical guidance. This is a resilience signal, not just a symptom report.*

> **"Personal trial and error."**

*Evidences complete absence of structured guidance in strategy development. No clinician, no peer community, no app — just iterative self-experimentation over an adult lifetime. This stands in stark contrast to younger participants who at least found peer forums.*

> **"Difficult to use apps at work — employers may not like phone use."**

*Evidences a concrete, age-correlated contextual constraint that is entirely absent from younger participants' data. This respondent's primary site of ADHD impact (work) is also the site where digital tools are least accessible. Any touchpoint designed around daytime mobile use would effectively exclude this user during their hours of greatest need.*

### Theme D — Stated feature preferences and openness

> **"Would prefer an online hangout with goal-setting pairings. A task-sending app. A feed for learning."**

*Evidences articulate, unprompted feature preferences that align closely with the body-doubling/accountability mechanisms the research identifies as the most effective ADHD-compatible support. The respondent did not arrive at these preferences through app experience — they reasoned their way there from need. This validates the team's peer-driven collaborative direction from a user who has not yet been influenced by existing products.*

> **"Not sure about timers or website blockers."**

*Evidences appropriate scepticism toward coercive/restrictive tools. Combined with the workplace phone constraint, this signals a preference for ambient, social, and optional scaffolding over enforcement-based features — a direct design implication.*

## 3. Thematic Analysis

### Theme 1 — Late-life diagnosis as reckoning, not revelation

For this respondent, diagnosis arrives not as an explanation of recent difficulties but as a retrospective reframing of an entire adult lifetime. They have managed ADHD symptoms — task completion, multitasking, motivation — for 60+ years, developing workarounds entirely alone. The emotional weight of this is not explicitly stated but is implied throughout: strategies that "work" were found through "personal trial and error" rather than any support system. The diagnosis does not immediately change daily function (they are unmedicated and await medication); it changes *meaning*. What the post-diagnostic window means for this respondent is therefore distinct from what it means for a 29-year-old: it is the beginning of a reinterpretation, not a new problem to solve.

### Theme 2 — Systemic abandonment at every stage

This respondent received no support at any point in the journey — not during the wait, not at diagnosis, not now. They were not told about Right to Choose; they found it themselves. They were not contacted during a year of NHS waiting; they made the call. Every step was self-initiated. This is systemic abandonment made concrete and sequential, and it is doubly striking because the respondent is 60+: they have navigated the NHS for decades and still found the system opaque and passive. The "7 years is a joke" quote is not anger at a single data point — it is the outlet for a year of proactive navigation in the face of institutional inertia.

### Theme 3 — Work as the epicentre of functional difficulty (and a design exclusion zone)

The workplace is named explicitly as the setting of greatest disruption, and it is simultaneously the setting where the most obvious digital interventions are inaccessible. This is a design tension unique to this respondent cohort (older, employed adults): the highest-need context is also the least reachable by a mobile app. Any solution targeting this persona must either function *outside* working hours (morning/evening scaffolding) or be invisible enough during work hours not to trigger employer scrutiny — which likely rules out most existing ADHD apps.

### Theme 4 — Autonomous strategy development as both resilience and evidence of neglect

The respondent has independently arrived at strategies (list-making with completion satisfaction, exercise, general "doing more") that are empirically supported for ADHD symptom management. This is remarkable and represents decades of adaptive problem-solving. But it also reveals the total absence of any guided support: had there been structured peer connection or clinical guidance at any earlier point, the strategies may have come sooner, been more comprehensive, or prevented compounding secondary effects. The resilience is real; the cost of that resilience — the decades of fumbling without a map — is also real.

### Theme 5 — Reasoned feature preferences from a non-app-user

Despite having used no ADHD apps, this respondent articulates specific, coherent feature preferences: online hangout with goal-setting pairings, task-sending functionality, a learning feed. These preferences were arrived at through reasoning from need, not from product experience. They align almost exactly with the team's body-doubling and peer-accountability direction. This is strong design validation from someone whose preferences are uncorrupted by prior product exposure — and it points toward a feature set that transcends age cohort, suggesting these mechanisms may be broadly wanted, not just by the younger participants who have explored similar tools.

### Theme 6 — The medication-pending threshold

This respondent sits in a unique transitional position: diagnosed but not yet medicated. They are not in the diagnostic queue (the wait is over), but they are not yet in the medicated-management phase that other post-diagnosis participants are in. This is a compressed, liminal window the team has not seen from other participants. It raises a design question: what does a person need in the days or weeks between diagnosis and first medication? The answer from this data is: structured information (the learning feed preference), social accountability (goal-setting pairings), and practical task support (task-sending app) — precisely the features they named.

## 4. Distinctive Contribution

### What uniquely surfaces from the 60+ unmedicated respondent

**Age as a qualitative amplifier.** Every younger participant has experienced ADHD for a portion of their life; this respondent has experienced it for 60+ years. This means the "gap" between diagnosis and understanding is not a matter of months but of decades. The emotional stakes of the post-diagnostic window are therefore qualitatively different: it is not just "what do I do now" but "what does this mean for everything that came before." The team's other interviewees received diagnosis with years of life still ahead in which to reorganise; this respondent is reorganising an already-built life.

**Unmedicated baseline.** Among the *working adult* participants in the dataset, every other respondent is (or has been) medicated. This respondent is the only working-adult participant whose post-diagnostic experience is being navigated entirely without pharmacological support — meaning their coping strategies, tool preferences, and support needs are not confounded by the symptom-moderating effect of medication. (A parallel unmedicated case exists in the student cohort — see `theo.md` — but Theo is unmedicated by *choice* (cost-driven opt-out) rather than by virtue of being in the diagnosis-to-first-prescription gap.) What this respondent says they need is what they actually need, unaided.

**The workplace constraint as an underrepresented design problem.** No other participant identified employer surveillance of phone use as a barrier to ADHD tools. This is an age-correlated workplace norm that older employed adults are disproportionately subject to, and it surfaces a design assumption — that the primary user interface is a smartphone accessible during the working day — that may systematically exclude a segment of the target population.

**Self-navigated R2C as lived evidence of information asymmetry.** This respondent discovered Right to Choose independently. They are an example of someone who successfully navigated the system, not because the system made it easy, but because they happened to search in the right place. They represent the better-outcome end of a luck-dependent continuum — which is precisely the inequity the problem statement identifies ("rely on luck — whether they happen to find the right peer community").

## 5. Connection to Locked Problem Scope

The locked scope is: *UK adults in the ADHD diagnostic queue and the first 12 months post-diagnosis.*

This respondent was, at time of interview, sitting at the exact boundary: the diagnostic queue is behind them (just received diagnosis); the first 12 months of the post-diagnosis window is in front of them. They are the only participant who is at the **diagnosis-to-first-medication** transition specifically — Daniel is medicated but currently facing supply disruption (a different liminal state), and Theo is medication-opt-out — making this respondent uniquely positioned at the literal opening of the medicated post-diagnosis window the team is designing for.

**Supports the scope:**
- Confirms the 3-year void (7 years in their area; over a year actually elapsed even with R2C navigation).
- Confirms that diagnosis is received without structured follow-on support ("no" support at any stage).
- Confirms peer/online resources as the only information source ("mostly reading online").
- Confirms that the post-diagnosis window is characterised by need for practical daily scaffolding, not clinical content.

**Nuances the scope:**
- The "first 12 months post-diagnosis" is not a uniform experience. For someone who is 60+ and awaiting medication, the opening of that window looks different from a 29-year-old who is already medicated and managing. The team should treat the window as having *sub-phases* (diagnosis-to-medication, medication-to-adjustment, adjustment-to-self-management) rather than as monolithic.
- The workplace constraint reveals that the post-diagnosis window for older employed adults may require a different delivery mechanism than the mobile-first design that works for students. The scope is right; the design may need to flex for this persona.
- This respondent's *readiness* to engage (articulate feature preferences, awareness of ADHD YouTube content, proactive system navigation) suggests that people who arrive at diagnosis having already done extensive self-research are a distinct sub-group within the first-12-months scope — they may need less orientation content and more structured peer connection immediately.

**Challenges the scope:**
- The 12-month cutoff is arbitrary from this respondent's perspective. The reinterpretation of a 60+ year life does not complete in 12 months. The team should note — at least for this persona — that the post-diagnostic support need is likely longer than 12 months, even if the product's primary design focus remains on the opening window.

## 6. Persona Signals

These signals anchor a **"Late-Life Diagnosis"** persona — distinct from the younger worker and student archetypes in the dataset. This persona is likely to be common: NHS adult ADHD diagnosis rates are rising fastest in middle and older age groups, and the diagnostic surge post-pandemic skews toward adults who were missed in childhood.

| Attribute | Signal from this respondent |
|---|---|
| **Self-reliance as default mode** | Strategies developed entirely through personal trial and error; no expectation of external support; proactively navigated Right to Choose without guidance. Persona is highly autonomous but not by preference — by necessity. |
| **Work is the problem domain, not school or social life** | Disruption is explicitly workplace-centred (organisation, sequential task completion). Unlike student participants, there is no academic deadline structure to substitute for self-regulation. |
| **Phone/app access is constrained during peak-need hours** | Employer culture limits visible phone use during work. Digital touchpoints must be usable before/after work, or be desk-based (browser), or require minimal interaction during the day (e.g., async task-sending from a partner). |
| **Collaborative accountability is the preferred mechanism** | Unprompted preference for online hangouts with goal-setting pairings and task-sending functionality — this is body-doubling logic arrived at independently. Not interested in enforcement tools (blockers, timers). |
| **Information diet is self-curated and online** | Relies on online reading and YouTube (ADHD-specific creator content). A learning feed would formalise and improve what they are already doing informally. |
| **Emotional register: measured, matter-of-fact, quietly outraged** | Describes 60+ years of undiagnosed life and systematic NHS failure in plain, dry language. The outrage in "7 years is a joke" is compressed, not dramatic. The persona is not fragile or overwhelmed — they are competent and frustrated. |

## 7. Pitch-Ready Evidence

The following quotes and observations are short enough and strong enough to land in a 3-minute M1 pitch without explanation.

**1. "7 years is a joke."**
Use case: the single most economical piece of evidence for the scale of the diagnostic void. Four words. No context needed in a pitch setting — the audience immediately grasps that a seven-year wait for a healthcare diagnosis is indefensible. Pairs well with the NHS workforce statistic and the 3-year void framing.

**2. "No [support during the waiting period]."**
Use case: answers the implicit question — "but surely patients get *something* while they wait?" — with a single word. Use as a direct response to a hypothetical NHS leaflet objection, or as punctuation after describing the wait length. Its brevity is its power.

**3. "Would prefer an online hangout with goal-setting pairings. A task-sending app. A feed for learning."**
Use case: a participant who has used *no* ADHD apps, arrived at feature preferences identical to the team's design direction through reasoning from lived need. This is demand-side validation that is uncorrupted by prior product experience. Use in the "audience and what they need" box of the pitch.

**4. "Most disrupted at work — requires organisation and completing tasks in sequence."**
Use case: humanises the impact of ADHD in the professional domain. Unlike a student's exam deadline, a working adult's sequential task requirement is permanent and continuous. Grounds the problem in economic productivity terms if the pitch needs to speak to a wider impact narrative.

## 8. Design Implications

### Implication 1 — Design for the before-and-after-work window, not the working day

This respondent's primary difficulty (work) is also their primary app-inaccessible zone. A product that requires active engagement during business hours will fail this persona. Design for **evening check-ins, morning preparation rituals, and asynchronous task-sending** so that the tool's support is felt during the day without requiring screen time during it. Consider a push-notification or calendar-integration model that delivers scaffolding to a work inbox or watch rather than requiring a phone to be opened.

### Implication 2 — Prioritise body-doubling and peer-pairing over individual productivity features

The respondent independently requested goal-setting pairings and a task-sending app — both of which are social/collaborative features, not individual productivity tools. They are sceptical of timers and blockers. This reinforces the design direction established by the broader evidence set: co-presence and accountability are the mechanism; enforcement and restriction are not. For this persona specifically, the social layer should be positioned as a professional peer-support arrangement (matching the language of their workplace context) rather than a student study-group.

### Implication 3 — Provide a structured learning feed as a distinct feature, not a by-product

"Mostly reading online" and "a feed for learning" both indicate that self-directed online research is this respondent's primary information strategy — and that they would value a curated version of it. A raw list of Reddit posts or YouTube links would reproduce what they already have. What is missing is *curation and structure*: ADHD-relevant content organised by theme (workplace strategies, understanding diagnosis, medication questions) rather than by chronology or popularity. This is a low-engineering but high-value feature for the late-life-diagnosis persona.

### Implication 4 — Make onboarding reflect the reinterpretation experience, not just orientation

For a 60+ newly diagnosed adult, the opening of the post-diagnosis window is primarily an act of *retrospective reframing*, not just forward planning. An onboarding flow that asks "what are your goals?" before acknowledging the emotional weight of a late-in-life diagnosis will feel jarring. Design onboarding to hold space for the reckoning ("you've been doing this without knowing, and that makes sense") before pivoting to practical scaffolding. This is a tone and sequence design decision, not a feature decision.

### Implication 5 — Do not assume smartphone as the primary interface for older employed users

The workplace phone constraint, combined with the age profile, points toward **browser-based access** (accessible on a work computer during a break, or on a home computer in the evening) as a more realistic interface for this persona than a native mobile app. The multi-user web/mobile requirement in the technical spec (CONCEPT_MAP.md §1) is consistent with this: the web app mode should be designed as a fully functional primary interface, not a secondary fallback for the mobile app.
