# Interview Analysis — Medicated Student 1

> DRP 2026 | Imperial DoC × RCA | Option C: UK adults in the ADHD diagnostic queue and the first 12 months post-diagnosis.

---

## 1. Respondent Profile

**Demographics & context:** University student (age and gender not specified in transcript), currently living partly on campus and partly at home. Speaks from the perspective of someone who has recently navigated the diagnostic system independently, without parental or institutional prompting.

**Diagnosis status:** Diagnosed via right-to-choose referral through their GP, followed by a private assessment at reduced cost. Timeline: approximately 3–4 months from GP visit to diagnosis — unusually fast relative to the NHS default wait, achieved by actively leveraging the right-to-choose pathway.

**Medication status:** Medicated. Reports medication is helpful across most areas but explicitly states "it does not fix anything." Uses supplementary coping strategies (deep breathing, coffee/tea) to manage medication crashes.

**Route to diagnosis:** Self-identified. Did not recognise ADHD symptoms during school; the need for independent organisation at university made symptoms legible for the first time. Discovery was through personal reflection and trial, not clinical flagging or family recognition.

**DSA status:** Chose not to apply for Disabled Students' Allowance. Rationale: the support offered through DSA did not feel like it would address their actual difficulties.

**Key personal context:** Father models timer-based time management — one of the few coping strategies the respondent uses consistently. The respondent operates in a context where the "overdiagnosed" public discourse is ambient, creating internalised guilt when tasks feel disproportionately hard. University institutional support was experienced as difficult to locate and somewhat disconnected.

## 2. Key Verbatim Quotes

### Theme A: The diagnostic pathway

> "Went to GP, referral via right-to-choose, private diagnosis without high cost. Roughly 3–4 months."

*Evidences that the right-to-choose route can dramatically compress the diagnostic timeline, but requires the patient to know it exists — the route is latent, not surfaced by institutions.*

> "Self-identified. Did not notice symptoms at school; became apparent when organising independently."

*Evidences the university transition as a critical symptom-onset trigger: the removal of external structure (teachers, timetables, parents) is what makes ADHD visible. This has persona-building implications — late self-identification in early adulthood is a distinct pathway.*

---

### Theme B: Institutional support failure

> "Did not apply for DSA — did not feel support offered would help."

*Evidences a rational trust deficit in institutional accommodations: the respondent made a considered judgment that the formal system would not address their specific lived difficulties. This is not ignorance of DSA — it is a deliberate opt-out. Critically, it means this respondent received almost no formal support during or after diagnosis.*

> "University support was hard to find."

*Evidences a discoverability failure in institutional support infrastructure, distinct from a quality failure. Even where support exists, the friction of locating it is prohibitive for an ADHD brain.*

> "Good in principle but could be refined. Feels like a disconnect. Difficult to communicate with third parties."

*Evidences that the respondent holds a nuanced, not wholly negative, view of institutions — they see the structural intent as reasonable but the execution as disconnected from actual user needs. "Disconnect" and "difficult to communicate" are design-legible failure modes.*

---

### Theme C: Tool failure modes

> "Finds existing tools too annoying or distracting to use consistently. Distracted by full feeds — prefers cleaner interfaces."

*Evidences a specific and actionable interaction failure: the friction is not with the concept of digital tools but with their UX density. Full-feed interfaces that demand engagement create the very distraction that the tool is meant to overcome. This is a concrete design constraint.*

> "Pomo Focus — knowing there is an end helps."

*Evidences the psychological mechanism behind timed work: uncertainty about endpoint is a core ADHD anxiety. The value proposition of any timer or session-based tool is temporal containment, not productivity maximisation.*

> "Used ChatGPT for planning (meal plans, running schedules)."

*Evidences a DIY workaround: the respondent is using a general-purpose LLM for structured personal planning because no domain-specific tool meets their needs with sufficient flexibility and low enough friction. Noteworthy that the use cases span life domains beyond work (nutrition, exercise).*

---

### Theme D: Stigma and internal guilt

> "Notes the problem of those who are not aware of ADHD symptoms — including the 'overdiagnosed' perception, which creates guilt when things feel harder without visible cause."

*Evidences the internalisation of a public discourse. The respondent does not simply encounter external stigma — they absorb the "overdiagnosed" narrative and apply it to themselves as self-doubt. This is a compounding dynamic: ADHD already produces emotional dysregulation, and the guilt loop adds a second, socially-produced layer of psychological burden.*

---

### Theme E: What actually helps

> "People around them who understand."

*Evidences that informal social understanding — not formal clinical support — is the primary felt source of meaningful help. Brief, but one of the strongest signals in the dataset for the peer-driven design direction.*

> "Helps in most aspects but does not fix anything."

*Evidences a mature, unsentimental understanding of medication's role. This respondent does not over-attribute relief to medication, which means they are actively seeking and maintaining non-pharmacological strategies — they are a rich informant for coping ecology.*

## 3. Thematic Analysis

### Theme 1: Self-recognition at the university transition

This respondent did not receive an early diagnosis — ADHD became legible only when external scaffolding (school timetables, parental oversight) was removed and self-organisation became required. This positions the university transition as both a diagnostic trigger and a vulnerability window. The pattern is significant: the same structural change that makes ADHD visible also removes the routine supports that were inadvertently masking it. The respondent thus enters university simultaneously discovering their diagnosis and losing the environmental compensations they had relied on unknowingly.

### Theme 2: Rational rejection of institutional support

The decision not to apply for DSA is not passive; it is an active, reasoned judgment that the available support would not address the actual problem. This theme surfaces a gap between what institutions offer (time extensions, note-taking services) and what this respondent actually needs (something more suited to executive function, planning, and emotional regulation). The additional observation that university support was "hard to find" layers a discoverability problem on top of a relevance problem — two separate failure modes that each independently reduce uptake.

### Theme 3: Tool failure as a design constraint, not a user deficit

The respondent's engagement with productivity tools (Pomo Focus, website blockers, physical timer, ChatGPT) demonstrates an active and experimental approach to self-management. Their abandonment of existing organiser apps is not due to lack of effort. The failure mode is specific: full-feed interfaces create distraction; the tools are "annoying" in ways that break the already-fragile habit loop. This is user-legible evidence that the failure sits with the tool design, not with the user's motivation.

### Theme 4: The "overdiagnosed" guilt loop

The respondent explicitly names the "overdiagnosed" public perception as a source of internal guilt when difficulties feel disproportionate to apparent cause. This is a psychologically sophisticated observation: stigma is not just an external social force but an internalised self-evaluative one. For an individual who self-identified rather than being flagged by a clinician or teacher, this creates a particular vulnerability — without an external authority confirming the validity of their diagnosis, the "maybe I'm just making excuses" doubt has more purchase.

### Theme 5: Coping ecology built without institutional input

The respondent's coping strategies (timers learned from father, in-the-moment exercise, consistent routine, ChatGPT planning, physical timer gadget) were developed entirely through personal trial-and-error and family modelling — not through any clinical, educational, or institutional pathway. This represents a significant investment of self-directed effort to build a functional coping ecosystem with zero professional guidance. The ecology works partially, but is precarious: it depends on the respondent continuously maintaining motivation to experiment and adapt.

### Theme 6: Medication as partial relief, not solution

The respondent's framing of medication — "helps in most aspects but does not fix anything" — positions it accurately as a symptom moderator rather than a treatment. They supplement medication with caffeine management (coffee/tea to offset crashes) and deep breathing. This multi-modal coping pattern, where medication is one component among several, is important context for design: a touchpoint aimed only at the pre-medication period would miss this respondent's ongoing need for non-pharmacological support even post-diagnosis and post-medication initiation.

## 4. Distinctive Contribution

This respondent's most distinctive contribution to the dataset is the **self-identified, institutionally-opted-out student** perspective: someone who navigated diagnosis quickly via right-to-choose, then deliberately declined DSA because the support on offer did not map to their actual difficulties — a rational rejection, not a disengagement. No other participant in the interview pool combines fast diagnosis, active self-management, and a principled non-take-up of formal accommodation.

This surfaces two things no other participant surfaces as clearly:

**First, the relevance gap in institutional support.** DSA exists. This respondent knew about it. They chose not to use it. That is a qualitatively different problem from the access barriers other participants describe — it is a product-market mismatch between the available support and this user group's real needs.

**Second, the internalised "overdiagnosed" guilt loop in a self-diagnosed individual.** Because this respondent identified their own ADHD without being flagged by a teacher, clinician, or parent, they have no external authority to anchor the legitimacy of their diagnosis. The ambient cultural discourse that ADHD is overdiagnosed then finds unusually fertile ground: there is no counter-narrative installed by an earlier clinical encounter. This produces guilt that is structurally different from — and potentially more persistent than — the stigma described by respondents who were diagnosed young.

## 5. Connection to Locked Problem Scope

**Option C scope:** UK adults in the ADHD diagnostic queue and the first 12 months post-diagnosis.

**Supports:**

- The respondent is post-diagnosis and within the early post-diagnosis window that Option C targets. Their experience confirms that medication initiation does not resolve the need for structured everyday support — corroborating the "post-diagnosis cliff" insight and the rationale for extending scope to 12 months post-diagnosis rather than stopping at the diagnostic event.
- The finding that peer understanding ("people around them who understand") is the most meaningful support source corroborates Insight 3 (peer community is the de facto working layer) across a different demographic sub-group.
- The tool failure modes corroborate Insight 5 (generic productivity tools fail because they're not built for the brain using them) with specific, actionable UX detail: full-feed interfaces and friction-heavy setups break usage consistency.

**Challenges / nuances:**

- This respondent's 3–4 month diagnostic pathway via right-to-choose is dramatically shorter than the NHS default described by other participants. This challenges the assumption that all Option C users will have had a long, unsupported wait — the in-queue experience for this participant was brief. The relevant void for them was not pre-diagnostic (they moved through that fast) but post-diagnostic: they found themselves with a diagnosis and medication but no structured support for the everyday living challenges.
- The DSA opt-out introduces a nuance for scope design: some users in this window are not using formal institutional support not because they cannot access it, but because it does not address their actual problem. A peer-driven digital touchpoint needs to be designed for users who have already made an informed judgment that generic accommodations don't help them.

**Overall:** The respondent fits Option C clearly and adds evidence weight to the post-diagnosis arc of the problem rather than the waiting-room arc. They strengthen the case that the 12-month post-diagnosis window is genuinely underserved, even for users who reached diagnosis relatively quickly.

## 7. Pitch-Ready Evidence

These four observations are short, concrete, and quotable for the M1 elevator pitch.

1. **"Did not apply for DSA — did not feel support offered would help."** — A university student who knew formal accommodations existed and still opted out. Institutions can provide support that users rationally judge as irrelevant to their actual difficulties. This is a relevance problem, not an access problem.

2. **"Finds existing tools too annoying or distracting to use consistently. Distracted by full feeds — prefers cleaner interfaces."** — Direct user testimony that the current tool landscape fails at the interaction level: the tools designed to help become the source of distraction. Not a motivation deficit — a design deficit.

3. **"People around them who understand"** as the most meaningful support source — even in a university context with institutional support available. Peer understanding outperforms formal provision. Corroborates the team's peer-driven design direction with a second independent student data point.

4. **Used ChatGPT for meal plans and running schedules** — a university student using a general LLM as a personal life planner because no purpose-built tool meets their needs with sufficient flexibility and low friction. A clear signal that the demand for structured everyday support exists and is unmet by current offerings.

## 8. Design Implications

### Implication 1: Full-feed interfaces are contraindicated

The respondent's explicit statement that full feeds cause distraction, and their expressed preference for cleaner interfaces, is a concrete negative design constraint. Any touchpoint that surfaces a scrollable timeline of peer activity, notifications, or content will replicate the failure mode of existing tools. Interface design should default to the minimum visible state required for the current task — showing more only on deliberate user action, not by default.

### Implication 2: Temporal containment is a core UX primitive

The mechanism behind Pomo Focus that this respondent values is not productivity in the abstract — it is knowing there is an end. This should be designed in at the session level: every interaction mode (focus session, peer check-in, planning task) should have a clear, visible endpoint. Open-ended or perpetual interaction modes (infinite scroll, always-on chat) will not work for this user group.

### Implication 3: Tone must pre-emptively de-shame

The "overdiagnosed" guilt loop is not solved by information — it is a discourse the respondent has already internalised. A platform aimed at this population must communicate, in its tone, framing, and copy, that difficulty is expected, normal, and not evidence of illegitimacy. This means avoiding language that implies the user "should" be doing more or performing better, avoiding gamification mechanics that expose failure (streaks broken, goals missed), and making the default experience feel non-judgmental. For self-identified users without a strong clinical anchor for their diagnosis, this is especially critical.

### Implication 4: Discoverability of support pathways is itself a product problem

The respondent found university support "hard to find" — not non-existent. This suggests that even where relevant support does exist (including right-to-choose referral routes, peer communities, specialist strategies), its discoverability is a blocker. A touchpoint could add significant value simply by surfacing and curating existing resources in a low-friction, ADHD-compatible format — without needing to build all of the underlying content from scratch.

### Implication 5: Onboarding must account for opt-out of formal support

A meaningful proportion of the target population (evidenced here, and by Theo's concerns about employer disclosure) will arrive at the touchpoint having already decided that formal institutional routes are not for them. Onboarding flows that assume users want to be connected to DSA, NHS services, or university disability offices will create immediate friction with this sub-group. The platform should treat peer-driven and self-directed strategies as first-class pathways — not as fallbacks for those who "can't access" formal support.
