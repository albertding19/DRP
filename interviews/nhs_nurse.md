# Interview Analysis — NHS Nurse (System-Side Practitioner)

> Part of the DRP 2026 qualitative research corpus. Analysed against the locked
> problem scope: **Option C — UK adults in the ADHD diagnostic queue and the first
> 12 months post-diagnosis.**
>
> Note: this is a **practitioner respondent**, not a patient. The analysis structure
> is adapted accordingly — no persona section; instead, sections on Stakeholder
> Signals and System-Side Distinctive Contribution.

## 1. Respondent Profile

**Role:** NHS Nurse  
**Experience with ADHD / mental health patients:** 6–10 years  
**Survey submitted:** 20 May 2026  
**Perspective:** System-side — observes patient journeys from within the NHS, witnesses consequences of the backlog, and works under the same structural pressures the survey asks about  
**Notable context:** Responses are brief and compressed — a pattern consistent with overstretch. Where answers are parenthetically inferred (e.g., "conveyed throughout"), the survey capture notes that the substance was communicated implicitly across multiple responses rather than as a single direct statement. These are treated as valid data points but distinguished from explicit verbatim responses.

## 2. Key Verbatim Quotes

Quotes are given exactly as recorded. Parenthetical annotations in the source data are
included where the response was implicit rather than explicit. After each quote, an italicised
note records what insight the quote evidences.

### Theme A — Wait Times

> "Waiting list is somewhere between 3–5 years."

*Evidences the most severe wait-time estimate in the entire dataset. Two practitioners were surveyed; the other (Mental Health Practitioner, 1–5yr experience) gave a 3-year estimate independently. This nurse's "3–5 years" extends the upper bound to five years — giving the team a defensible range of 3–5 years backed by two independent system-side sources. Unlike a patient's estimate, a nurse's estimate is grounded in daily operational knowledge of the list, making it credible third-party evidence for the M1 pitch.*

---

### Theme B — Patient Consequences

> "Some lose the proper care they deserve, affecting family dynamics; frustration and anxiety develop."

*Evidences that the wait is not merely an inconvenience but a family-system event. The phrase "lose the proper care they deserve" carries a normative charge — this nurse is not describing a neutral administrative delay but a deprivation of something owed. "Affecting family dynamics" extends the impact beyond the individual patient to household-level harm — frustration and anxiety radiate outward to partners, parents, and children. This is the only direct reference in the practitioner corpus to family members as secondary victims of the backlog.*

> "Not being able to attend school, as mainstream schools do not accept them."

*Evidences the downstream exclusionary consequence of untreated ADHD during the wait. Mainstream school exclusion is a concrete, measurable harm: children and young people whose ADHD is undiagnosed and unsupported are removed from educational settings. This is outside the team's adult scope but is significant in two ways: it contextualises how early unaddressed ADHD shapes life trajectories by adulthood, and it signals that the nurse's patient population includes young people as well as adults — important for interpreting the generalisability of their other observations.*

> "(Implied yes) — patients and families are already fed up with the system and lack trust in staff."

*Evidences system-trust collapse as a compounding factor. Critically, this is a practitioner acknowledging — unprompted, about their own profession — that patients have lost confidence in the staff who are supposed to help them. This is not a patient complaint about a system; it is a nurse's honest appraisal of where patient-clinician relationships stand. It signals that any external digital touchpoint must be very careful not to position itself as an extension of that discredited system, or it will inherit the same trust deficit.*

---

### Theme C — System Pressures

> "More cost to the NHS."

*Evidences the economic burden of the backlog — given as both the consequence for the NHS today (knock-on effects question) and as the future trajectory (where is this heading). The doubling of this answer across two separate questions is not redundancy — it signals that cost escalation is the nurse's dominant frame for systemic harm. Untreated ADHD is more expensive to manage as co-morbidities accumulate (anxiety, substance use, school exclusion, employment breakdown) than early structured support would be. This is a value proposition for the team's pitch to stakeholders: a non-clinical support layer reduces downstream NHS cost.*

> "Not sufficient."

*Evidences a practitioner's direct assessment of the adequacy of current NHS ADHD and mental health care. Terse — but the brevity is itself informative: this nurse does not hedge, qualify, or offer a balanced view. Two words. The system is not sufficient.*

---

### Theme D — Practitioner Experience

> "Yes." [in response to: Do you and your colleagues feel overstretched?]

*Evidences workforce overstretch as an unambiguous practitioner-side reality. This is not patient-reported perception but direct self-report from inside the system. Combined with the "not sufficient" assessment and the cost-burden framing, it completes a picture of a practitioner who is operating in conditions of structural inadequacy while watching patient harm accumulate.*

> "Yes." [in response to: Are mental health patients overlooked or deprioritised compared to other healthcare areas?]

*Evidences structural deprioritisation of mental health within the NHS. Again, the answer is unhedged. Mental health patients — including the ADHD diagnostic population — are acknowledged by this practitioner to be receiving lower priority than other healthcare areas. This is system-side confirmation of what patient participants describe experiencing. The team can cite this as a practitioner validating, not merely echoing, the patient-side perception of being de-ranked within the health system.*

---

### Theme E — Prognosis

> "(From the experience patients share) — more cost to the NHS." [in response to: Where is this heading over the next few years if nothing changes?]

*Evidences a pessimistic but analytically specific forecast: without structural change, the backlog compounds into greater financial burden. The parenthetical note that this was "from the experience patients share" is also significant — it reveals that the nurse's forward-looking view is grounded in accumulated patient testimony, i.e., the nurse is synthesising what dozens or hundreds of patients have told them. This is not speculation; it is aggregated clinical observation converted into a system-level prognosis.*

> "ADHD and autism — getting the right treatment at the right time is more important." [in response to: What do patients most commonly misunderstand about ADHD?]

*Evidences the co-morbidity and misdiagnosis dimension. This is a practitioner flagging that patients commonly conflate ADHD and autism, and that the priority is temporal accuracy of the correct treatment — "at the right time" — over the diagnostic distinction itself. This has design implications: a touchpoint for people in the ADHD queue will encounter some users who may also be on an autism waiting list, or who have been bounced between diagnostic categories. The platform should not presuppose single-condition clarity.*

## 3. Thematic Analysis

### Theme 1 — Scale of Wait as a Structural, Not Administrative, Problem

The nurse's response "somewhere between 3–5 years" is not offered as a complaint — it is offered as a clinical fact. The phrasing "somewhere between" suggests even the nurse does not have a precise number, which itself evidences the scale: the wait is long enough that the range spans years, not weeks or months. Read alongside the MHP's independently given three-year estimate, the two practitioners converge on a 3–5 year range with consistency that makes the figure credible and citable. This theme is the nurse's single most valuable contribution to the quantitative-adjacent evidence layer the team needs for the M1 pitch.

### Theme 2 — Family System as a Unit of Harm

No patient participant named family dynamics as a primary harm. This nurse does. "Affecting family dynamics; frustration and anxiety develop" positions the ADHD waiting period as a household-wide crisis, not an individual experience. The clinical literature supports this — family members of adults with ADHD report elevated caregiver burden, relationship strain, and secondary anxiety — but it is absent from the patient interviews, likely because patients describe their own experience and not the systemic effects on those around them. The nurse's clinical vantage point allows them to see what the patient cannot self-report: the radial damage to the family unit.

### Theme 3 — School Exclusion as the Childhood Trace of the Same System Failure

The school-exclusion observation is nominally out of the team's adult scope but it is analytically significant for what it reveals about the longitudinal structure of the problem. Adults in the ADHD diagnostic queue in 2026 were, for the most part, children who were not diagnosed or excluded from school in the 1990s–2010s. The pipeline from undiagnosed childhood ADHD to adult diagnostic queue is the same system failure playing out across decades. The nurse's observation makes that pipeline visible in a way that no adult patient interview can — because the patients are already downstream. This does not expand the scope; it contextualises why the scope looks the way it does.

### Theme 4 — System-Trust Collapse as an Environmental Constraint

The nurse's acknowledgement that "patients and families are already fed up with the system and lack trust in staff" is structurally unusual: it is a practitioner reporting damage to their own profession's credibility with patients. This shapes the design environment in a specific way. The team's touchpoint enters a context where the NHS-adjacent institutional frame already carries negative valence for the target population. A tool that looks like an NHS extension, uses NHS branding, or presents itself as a clinical supplement will face that pre-existing trust deficit immediately. The reverse framing — explicitly non-clinical, peer-driven, independent of the system — is not just a design preference; it is a trust-recovery requirement.

### Theme 5 — NHS Cost Burden as a Systemic Self-Reinforcing Harm

The nurse cites "more cost to the NHS" twice — as the present knock-on effect and as the future trajectory. This doubling is notable because the nurse is not answering the same question twice: the first is backward-looking (current consequences), the second is forward-looking (five-year projection). The same answer for both directions indicates the nurse sees the cost burden as the structural constant, not a temporary condition. For the team, this opens a stakeholder messaging pathway: the touchpoint is not just good for patients, it is economically valuable to the NHS by reducing the downstream co-morbidity load that expensive acute services must absorb.

## 4. Distinctive Contribution

This practitioner makes four contributions that no other respondent in the corpus provides.

**(a) The highest wait-time estimate in the dataset — "3–5 years."**
The patient respondents gave individual anecdotal estimates (one patient discovered a 7-year wait in their area — a single-case outlier). The nurse gives a practitioner-level operational estimate of 3–5 years, independent of the MHP's 3-year figure. Together, the two practitioners establish a defensible, source-diverse range of 3–5 years that the team can cite as having been confirmed by NHS staff. This is the closest the team can get to a third-party statistic without published official NHS data and is essential for the M1 pitch.

**(b) The school-exclusion downstream effect — "mainstream schools do not accept them."**
No other respondent names educational exclusion as a witnessed consequence of the diagnostic backlog. This observation opens a view of the long-term trajectory of untreated ADHD that adult patient self-reports cannot reach. It also signals that the nurse's patient population includes younger patients whose parents are navigating the system alongside them — a secondary stakeholder group the team may not have fully accounted for.

**(c) The family-dynamics impact — "affecting family dynamics; frustration and anxiety develop."**
The patient corpus is self-focused, as expected: patients describe their own experience. The nurse is the only respondent to explicitly name the family as a secondary harm site. Partners, parents, and children of adults in the ADHD queue are affected — by the patient's frustration, by the patient's anxiety, and by the absence of support that would stabilise the household. This is the strongest single evidence point for including family members in the team's stakeholder map.

**(d) The system-trust collapse — "patients and families are already fed up with the system and lack trust in staff."**
This is the most professionally courageous observation in the practitioner data: a nurse acknowledging that patients have lost trust in the people supposed to help them. It does not come from a patient (who would be reporting their own experience) but from a clinician looking outward at their own system. It is a design-environment warning: the touchpoint operates in a low-trust context and must earn credibility independently of the NHS institutional frame.

## 5. Connection to Locked Problem Scope

This practitioner validates Option C from the system side. They confirm:

- The diagnostic-queue void exists and is 3–5 years long — directly corroborating the "3-year void" insight from the Define phase.
- The wait produces real harm — family dynamics, anxiety, frustration, loss of care entitlement — consistent with the problem statement's "worse outcomes for those who don't" find support.
- The system is not sufficient and practitioners are overstretched — confirming the root cause analysis that the gap is structural, not a temporary resource fluctuation.
- There is no clinical cavalry coming — the prognosis is "more cost" not "better care" — making the non-clinical peer-driven layer the only realistic near-term intervention.

**One nuancing tension with the adult scope:** The school-exclusion observation flags that at least some of this nurse's ADHD-related patient caseload is children and young people, not adults. The team's scope is UK adults. The nurse's observation is therefore partially out of scope as a direct evidence point — but it is not irrelevant. It contextualises the adult diagnostic queue as the downstream destination of a childhood diagnostic failure. Adults presenting in 2026 who were never diagnosed as children are, in part, the product of the same systemic avoidance this nurse witnesses in schools. The team should note this explicitly in the pitch as a demonstration of depth of understanding, while keeping the intervention focus on adults.

**No data from this nurse destabilises Option C.** The scope holds.

## 6. Stakeholder Signals

The nurse portrait that emerges from this survey is of a practitioner who is:

- Overstretched (explicitly confirmed)
- Working within a system they assess as "not sufficient"
- Watching patients and families lose trust in staff — including themselves
- Aware of the downstream cost consequences but unable to address them within their role
- Willing to be honest about systemic failure in a survey context

What does this suggest about the practitioner stakeholder group and their attitude toward a non-clinical digital touchpoint?

**Practitioners are potential quiet allies, not gatekeepers.** A nurse who acknowledges that "some lose the proper care they deserve" and that "patients and families are already fed up with the system" is not protective of the clinical monopoly on support — they are operating in a system they cannot defend. A well-framed digital touchpoint that demonstrably reduces patient distress, reduces family strain, and (by the nurse's own logic) reduces downstream NHS cost would likely be welcomed, not resisted, by practitioners in this position.

**The risk is not practitioner hostility — it is clinical framing.** If the touchpoint positions itself as a clinical supplement or a diagnostic aid, it will create scope conflict with practitioners and risk being dismissed as non-evidence-based. If it is clearly positioned as a peer-driven, everyday-living scaffold that explicitly does not replace clinical care, practitioners have no territorial reason to oppose it and have several practical reasons to signpost patients toward it.

**The trust collapse is a constraint, not an obstacle.** The nurse's observation that patients "lack trust in staff" means practitioners cannot simply recommend the touchpoint and have it immediately trusted. The touchpoint must build its own credibility — through peer voice, through design that acknowledges the NHS's failures rather than papering over them, and through a tone that respects what the patient has already been through.

## 8. Design Implications

### Implication 1 — The touchpoint must fill the gap the NHS explicitly cannot fill, not compete with it

The nurse confirms the NHS is "not sufficient," practitioners are overstretched, and mental health patients are deprioritised. This is not a system that will be fixed by the time the team's touchpoint launches. The design brief is therefore not "how do we help people while they wait for proper care" — it is "how do we provide the structured peer scaffolding that the NHS structurally cannot and will not provide." The distinction matters because it reframes the touchpoint from a stopgap to a primary service layer. **Design constraint:** every feature should be defensible as occupying a gap the NHS cannot fill, not as a clinical substitute that clinicians would need to validate.

### Implication 2 — The stakeholder map must include family members

This nurse is the only respondent to explicitly name family dynamics as a harm site and families as a trust-deficit group ("patients and families are already fed up"). The team's current stakeholder map (primary users: in-queue and first-year-post-diagnosis adults) should add partners, parents, and household members as secondary stakeholders. They are affected by the wait, affected by the patient's frustration and anxiety, and — if the touchpoint stabilises the patient — indirect beneficiaries. They may also be a route to onboarding: family members searching for support resources may be the first to encounter the touchpoint. **Design constraint:** the touchpoint should at minimum not exclude family members; at best it should have features that acknowledge their role without turning them into carers or co-therapists.

### Implication 3 — The touchpoint's framing must be explicitly independent of the NHS

The trust collapse the nurse describes — "patients and families are already fed up with the system and lack trust in staff" — means the touchpoint cannot trade on NHS credibility, because that credibility has been depleted. Any visual design, copy, or onboarding language that reads as NHS-adjacent will inherit the same trust deficit. The touchpoint must establish its own authority — through peer voice, through design that acknowledges what the system has failed to provide, and through explicit positioning as independent, non-clinical, and user-led. **Design constraint:** avoid language like "support your NHS journey," "complement your clinical care," or anything that positions the NHS as a trusted partner the touchpoint is working alongside. The framing should be: "built by people who know the system isn't working, for people who are living with that."

### Implication 4 — Reducing NHS cost burden is a B2B value proposition for stakeholder messaging

The nurse cites "more cost to the NHS" twice as the present and future consequence of the backlog. This creates a value proposition that goes beyond the patient experience: a non-clinical support layer that reduces anxiety, prevents avoidable co-morbidities, and stabilises family functioning will reduce the downstream demand on acute NHS services. The team should develop this as a parallel stakeholder messaging track — not the patient pitch ("this is for you") but the commissioner/policy/NHS-adjacent pitch ("this reduces your cost burden"). **Design constraint:** this value proposition does not affect the touchpoint's feature set, but it should be articulated in the M1 pitch alongside the patient-facing rationale.

### Implication 5 — The ADHD and autism co-occurrence flagged by the nurse suggests the touchpoint must not presuppose single-condition clarity in its users

The nurse's observation that patients commonly conflate ADHD and autism — and that "getting the right treatment at the right time is more important" — surfaces a clinical reality that the team's scope does not fully account for: many people in the ADHD diagnostic queue may also be awaiting autism assessment, or may have already received an autism diagnosis. A touchpoint designed exclusively around ADHD identity may create friction for users who are navigating dual or uncertain diagnoses. **Design constraint:** onboarding and community design should accommodate neurodivergent identity broadly rather than requiring users to have a confirmed or singular ADHD diagnosis. Peer pairing and community categorisation should allow for "suspected ADHD" and "ADHD + autism" rather than binary ADHD-confirmed/ADHD-not-confirmed groups.
