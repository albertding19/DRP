## 1. Respondent Profile

Daniel is an Imperial College London student who received an ADHD diagnosis via Imperial's own internal route — a consultation with someone at Imperial, followed by a two-hour assessment involving questions and surveys. The process itself was not clinically long per appointment, but cumulative waiting times across stages stretched the overall journey to several months. Daniel is currently medicated but is facing an acute medication-access crisis: he received written notice from the provider that "nothing will be able to be done in the near future." He grew up without any ADHD awareness or diagnosis, and describes a pre-diagnosis period characterised by feelings of inadequacy and not knowing why he was struggling. His lived experience spans the full arc of the locked problem scope — the extended diagnostic wait, the transition into medication, and now a system-failure moment that has effectively recreated the pre-medication vulnerability in the post-diagnosis window.

## 3. Thematic Analysis

### Theme 1 — Episodic but Severe Stigma

Daniel's account of stigma has a specific texture: it is not omnipresent, but when it surfaces it goes immediately to personhood ("treat you as less of a person"). This is distinct from mild social discomfort or awkward curiosity; it is a dignity-threatening event. The episodic, unpredictable quality — "normally fine but in cases where it's an issue" — means that stigma cannot be treated as a background risk to be noted and bracketed. A platform operating in this space must be designed with the assumption that users have experienced stigma in the past and that any feature triggering disclosure (public posts, visible diagnoses, visible community membership) re-exposes them to it. Destigmatisation is not an optional tone consideration; it is a structural safety requirement.

### Theme 2 — The Medication-Access Cliff as a Post-Diagnosis System Failure

Daniel's current situation is distinct from every other participant's: he is already diagnosed, already knows medication helps, and has had medication removed due to a supply failure communicated to him in writing. He is in the post-diagnosis window of the locked scope but has been functionally returned to pre-medication conditions through system failure rather than personal choice. This creates a distinct user state that the team's problem scope did not explicitly anticipate: the "stranded post-diagnosis medicated user." The medication email is the most concrete institutional evidence in the dataset that the post-diagnosis cliff (Insight 2) is not merely about thin clinical follow-up but can include active loss of an intervention that was previously working.

### Theme 3 — The Pre-Diagnosis Invisibility-to-Inadequacy Pipeline

Daniel describes the pre-diagnosis period not as neutral ignorance but as actively self-blaming ignorance: he felt behind, he felt inadequate, and he "mostly just didn't know" why. The absence of a framework for understanding his experience meant the only available explanation was a personal failure narrative. He then extends this to primary and secondary education more broadly — if you have undiagnosed ADHD in school, you are "perceived as stupid or disorganised." The pipeline is: invisibility (no diagnosis) → misattribution (character flaw) → self-blame → compounding inadequacy. This is a developmental harm, and it explains why a platform that provides a framework for understanding ADHD before diagnosis is concluded has disproportionate value: it interrupts the pipeline before self-blame solidifies.

### Theme 4 — Peer Community as the Structural Inflection Point

University gave Daniel something he had never had: a dense peer group that included other people with ADHD. This was not a designed intervention — it was a side effect of social concentration. The effect was significant enough that he names it explicitly: "was really good cause shared struggles and can share different strategies." This mirrors the "peer community is where help actually comes from" insight but adds a before-and-after quality: Daniel can locate the specific moment (coming to uni) when peer community appeared. Before that moment, the community was absent. The implication for design is that the platform's job is to manufacture the equivalent of that inflection point for people who are not in a dense, ADHD-aware social environment — which is most of the target group.

### Theme 5 — Vicious Cycle as a Distinct Mechanism, Not Generic Procrastination

Daniel's account of academic avoidance has a specific loop structure: miss a session → fall behind → feel too behind to re-engage → skip to cope → fall further behind. This is not ordinary procrastination; it is a compounding collapse that has an identifiable entry point (the first skip) and a self-sealing quality (catching up feels impossible). Generic productivity tools offer reminders, to-do lists, and streaks — none of which interrupt the loop at its sealing point. What does interrupt it is exogenous re-engagement: someone else prompting you, a study partner who notices the absence, a peer accountability structure that lowers the re-entry cost. This is a precise mechanism that should drive feature design.

### Theme 6 — Explicit Medicated/Unmedicated Capability Contrast

Daniel is one of the few participants in a position to describe the capability gap between medicated and unmedicated states from direct experience, and he does so clearly: he "feel[s] less capable" off medication. This is not a background insight — it actively reframes the medication-shortage situation. The practical loss is not just discomfort; it is a measurable capability reduction in a student whose academic and daily functioning depends on that capability. The implication is that non-pharmacological supports (peer accountability, external structure, body-doubling) are not secondary options but necessary compensatory mechanisms during medication gaps — which, as Daniel's situation demonstrates, can arrive suddenly and through no fault of the user.

## 4. Distinctive Contribution

Daniel uniquely contributes four things to the dataset that no other participant provides in the same form:

**(a) Explicit stigma as a personhood attack.** Daniel uses the precise formulation "treat you as less of a person" — not as embarrassment or social awkwardness, but as a dehumanising event. This is the sharpest articulation of ADHD stigma in the dataset and is directly quotable in the pitch and in the HCD portfolio's framing of the "Protecting" design principle. It specifies the stakes of getting platform tone wrong.

**(b) The medication-shortage crisis documented by institutional communication.** The email from the provider stating "nothing will be able to be done in the near future" is a piece of evidence unlike anything else in the interview set: it is externally sourced, written, and institutional. It transforms the "post-diagnosis cliff" from a subjective complaint into an evidenced system event. For the M1 pitch, this is pitch-grade evidence — a concrete, verifiable consequence of the "diagnosis is a one-time event" insight.

**(c) The "vicious cycle" framing for untreated procrastination.** Where other participants describe procrastination in general terms, Daniel gives it a mechanism: a compounding avoidance loop with a specific entry point and self-sealing quality. This adds precision to Insight 5 (generic tools fail) because it explains *why* they fail — they do not address the re-entry cost of the loop. Peer accountability directly targets that re-entry cost.

**(d) The lived medicated/unmedicated capability contrast.** Because Daniel has experienced both states and is currently losing medication access, he can compare them directly. This transforms the abstract "medication helps" finding into a concrete delta — a capability loss that is measurable in his daily experience. It also makes the case for non-pharmacological peer scaffolding not as a nice-to-have but as a necessary resilience mechanism.

## 5. Connection to Locked Problem Scope

The locked scope covers "UK adults in the ADHD diagnostic queue and the first 12 months post-diagnosis." Daniel fits the post-diagnosis arm of this scope, but with a critical twist: he is experiencing a system-failure event that has functionally reopened the pre-medication vulnerability window. He is not in the queue; he is post-diagnosis. But medication withdrawal has effectively recreated the conditions of the unmedicated diagnostic period — reduced capability, increased reliance on external scaffolding — through no action of his own.

This means Daniel's experience tests the resilience of the locked scope's assumptions. The problem statement assumes that post-diagnosis means medication is stable and the gap is primarily about information and peer community. Daniel shows that medication stability cannot be assumed even within the first post-diagnosis period. A solution scoped to post-diagnosis users must therefore be designed to remain useful — and potentially become more critical — precisely when medication fails. This is a stress-test on the design, not a reason to extend the scope: it argues for building the peer-scaffolding and accountability layer robustly enough to function as a compensatory mechanism when the pharmacological layer collapses.

## 6. Persona Signals

Daniel could anchor an **"Imperial-route diagnosed student"** persona, characterised by:

1. **Diagnosis context:** University-internal route; faster than NHS but still multi-month; chosen deliberately because NHS timelines were known to be prohibitive. Represents the subset of users who had institutional access to a non-NHS pathway.

2. **Current system position:** Post-diagnosis, previously medicated, now in medication-access crisis. Sitting at the intersection of the post-diagnosis window and an active system failure — a compound vulnerability state.

3. **Capability self-awareness:** Strong, clear sense of the functional difference between medicated and unmedicated states; uses this awareness to identify non-pharmacological compensatory strategies (planning, external prompting, peer community) rather than passively waiting for medication to be restored.

4. **Peer-community orientation:** University was the first environment in which peer community with shared ADHD experience became available. Values it highly; actively names strategy-sharing as a benefit. Would be a strong participant in a structured peer platform rather than a passive consumer.

5. **Stigma posture:** Aware of stigma; willing to discuss it analytically rather than emotionally; advocates explicitly for reframing ADHD as a disability in educational settings. This is a user who would engage with — and contribute to — destigmatisation features.

6. **Compensation strategy profile:** External structure (planning), outward-directed social prompting ("prompt others to get him to do things"), peer community strategy-sharing. Does not rely primarily on self-initiated internal discipline — consistent with ADHD executive-function profiles and consistent with what the platform's accountability/body-doubling features should support.

## 8. Design Implications

### Implication 1 — Medication-supply-disruption resilience as a design requirement

Daniel's experience demonstrates that medication access can be withdrawn suddenly and officially, returning a previously medicated user to an unmedicated capability state without warning. The platform cannot assume medication stability in post-diagnosis users. When medication fails, peer scaffolding, external accountability, and body-doubling become the primary functional support layer — not a supplement to it. Design implication: the platform's accountability and co-presence features must be robust enough to carry a user's functioning during medication gaps. They should not be framed as enhancements to pharmacological treatment but as independent resilience mechanisms.

### Implication 2 — Awareness and destigmatisation in earlier-life contexts (school)

Daniel specifically names primary and secondary school as the environment where undiagnosed ADHD does the most harm — "might be perceived as stupid or disorganised" — and frames ADHD as a disability that should be treated as one. While school-age diagnosis is explicitly out of scope for the platform, Daniel's retrospective account argues for building features that help adult users reframe their earlier experiences (inadequacy, feeling "behind") as disability-mediated rather than character-driven. This is a destigmatisation function the platform can serve for adults who went undiagnosed through school: providing a framework that makes sense of those earlier years and interrupts ongoing self-blame.

### Implication 3 — Accountability and body-doubling as first-class features

Daniel's active coping strategy includes "prompt others to get him to do things" — a precise description of outward-directed accountability prompting. Combined with his description of the vicious avoidance cycle, this points to a specific feature need: not reminders (which place the re-engagement burden back on the user) but peer-initiated prompting (where another user notifies, checks in, or co-works with the participant). Body-doubling and accountability-partner features are not secondary gamification but the primary intervention mechanism for Daniel's loop. The platform should enable users to designate partners who are expected to initiate contact — reversing the conventional notification model.

### Implication 4 — University and institutional community as a critical support layer to replicate

Daniel's peer community appeared only at university — and he is explicit that it did not exist before. The platform's core value proposition for users who do not have that community (including in-queue adults, non-student adults, people in regions with low ADHD peer density) is to manufacture the same structural inflection point. This has implications for onboarding design: the platform should connect new users to peers within the first session, not after profile completion or a waiting period. The community is the product; everything else is scaffolding for it.

### Implication 5 — Transparent service-navigation support

Daniel chose the Imperial route because he knew the NHS would take "far far longer" — but this knowledge was not provided to him by a service; he acquired it informally. Many users in the diagnostic queue do not know that university services, private routes, or Right to Choose exist as alternatives. The platform has an opportunity to provide transparent, up-to-date service navigation — including medication-shortage signposting — so that users can make informed choices about their own pathways rather than discovering alternatives by chance. This is especially relevant when medication supply fails: users need to know what alternatives exist and how to access them without waiting for a clinician to initiate the conversation.
