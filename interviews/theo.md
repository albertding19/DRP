## 1. Respondent Profile

**Age / year group:** First-year Imperial College London student (source note reads "Forst year adhd" — interpreted as first-year undergraduate, though the phrase could alternatively mean first year since ADHD diagnosis; team to confirm)  
**Diagnosis status:** Formally diagnosed with ADHD; route was Imperial's internal disability-services process followed by an external provider for the final assessment step  
**Medication status:** Has not tried medication; explicitly does not want to pursue it due to cost concerns — unmedicated by choice  
**Insurance:** Had private insurance coverage, which covered the external component (which was more expensive than the Imperial-managed route but easier to navigate)  
**NHS awareness:** Uncertain whether NHS testing is available past a certain age; did not pursue the NHS route  
**Presentation context:** First encountered the idea of having ADHD through jokes from peers ("told jokingly by lots of people that he's autistic or has adhd") before being taken seriously by similar peers and deciding to get tested  
**Key personal context:** Currently managing ADHD without medication using a self-developed study strategy modelled loosely on Pomodoro; found a body-doubling/social-study approach more effective than a dedicated solo-focus app (Flown); reports stigma concerns in both personal identity and professional (employment) contexts; is within the first 12 months post-diagnosis and actively navigating what that label means for his sense of self.

## 2. Key Verbatim Quotes

### Theme A — The Imperial diagnostic access bottleneck

> "Drops biweekly at noon on Monday and fills out very fast … filled out within 6 minutes."
> *(unedited interview notes)*

*Evidences a structural access problem that is invisible from outside: even when an institutional route nominally exists, it operates as a timed release that fills in under six minutes — effectively a lottery for students who happen to be available at the right moment on the right day. This is qualitatively different from a long wait; it is an arbitrary exclusion mechanism dressed as a service.*

> "Wait times — really hard to get initial chat w disability officers."
> *(unedited interview notes)*

*Evidences that the bottleneck is not unique to Theo — "really hard" points to a systemic rather than incidental access failure. The Imperial route is positioned as an accessible alternative to NHS, yet it replicates scarcity at its first step.*

---

### Theme B — Employment stigma as a structural silencer

> "Concerned that support from employers would be detrimental to success since only contact w employers is in recruitment context hence wary to supply that information."
> *(unedited interview notes)*

*Evidences a downstream effect of stigma that is specific to young adults entering the labour market: the fear is not abstract but strategic — disclosure to employers is perceived as a professional liability, particularly during recruitment. This is a distinctly different stigma context from the social or family stigma reported by other participants, and it has direct design implications (any platform requiring workplace disclosure would be unusable for this user).*

---

### Theme C — Self-extrapolated coping strategy as adaptive expertise

> "W revision does very long durations but extremely spaced ie 9 mins studying one minute off for hours."
> *(unedited interview notes)*

*Evidences that Theo has, without formal guidance, converged on a working strategy by observing his own concentration patterns and tuning parameters empirically. This is not a failure to self-manage — it is self-management working, but at significant personal cost (the strategy was entirely self-derived, trial-and-error, with no support structure to shortcut the process).*

> "This strategies similar to pomodoro and says he extrapolated this to his personal one. Parameters were just eyeballed. Whenever concentration wanes takes a break."
> *(unedited interview notes)*

*Evidences the gap between what structured support could have provided (an informed starting framework) and what he actually received (nothing — he had to eyeball his own parameters). The word "extrapolated" signals a mathematically-oriented mind solving an engineering problem about himself, which is a strong persona signal and a pitch-ready observation.*

---

### Theme D — Failure of solo-focus technology; effectiveness of body-doubling

> "Tried briefly found it an encumbrance and switched to j studying w other ppl in shared ppl."
> *(unedited interview notes; refers to Flown, a companion study tool)*

*Evidences a clean A/B comparison: tried a dedicated ADHD-oriented digital focus tool, found it added friction rather than removed it, and replaced it with an informal co-presence arrangement. The word "encumbrance" is precise — it describes a tool that cost more than it returned.*

> "Accountability enables more effective studying."
> *(unedited interview notes)*

*The single most design-relevant statement in this interview. It names the mechanism that works — not features, not gamification, not AI suggestions, but the social accountability of another person being present. This corroborates the 29yr medicated worker's data on body-doubling and strengthens the case for co-presence as a core product mechanic.*

---

### Theme E — Post-diagnosis identity reframing

> "Nice to put a label on some of his oddities."
> *(unedited interview notes; described as a direct quote)*

*Evidences the diagnostic moment as psychologically generative — not merely administrative. The word "oddities" carries a lifetime of self-perception before any explanation was available. The label is welcomed not as stigma but as resolution.*

> "Nice to have a technical train of thought for why he does certain things less efficiently."
> *(unedited interview notes)*

*Evidences a specific identity-reframing pattern: Theo contextualises his ADHD through an engineering or mathematical lens ("technical train of thought"), recasting inefficiency not as personal failure but as a parameter in a system he can now reason about. This is a potentially very powerful framing for a product designed for this population — and a persona signal that points toward users who want mechanistic self-understanding, not just emotional support.*

---

### Theme F — Stigma, inadequacy, and emotional processing

> "Individual personally bore a stigma to both adhd and autism pre diagnosis. Even afterwards says a little bit. Sad when diagnosed and took some time to realise this didn't change him. Feelings of inadequacy."
> *(unedited interview notes)*

*Evidences that stigma is not resolved by diagnosis — it is complicated by it. The sadness at diagnosis and the time needed to reach the realisation "this didn't change him" is a named emotional arc that the post-diagnosis period must accommodate. Design that skips this transitional processing (moving straight to productivity) would miss where many newly diagnosed users actually are.*

## 3. Thematic Analysis

### Theme 1 — Institutional access bottlenecks replicate NHS scarcity at smaller scale

Theo did not wait years in an NHS queue, but he encountered a structurally similar problem inside Imperial's disability services: a biweekly drop-in that fills within six minutes. This is the university-route equivalent of the three-to-five-year NHS wait — not a duration problem, but an access structure problem. A drop-in slot that disappears in under six minutes is only usable by students who are (a) aware of it, (b) available at exactly noon on a Monday, and (c) fast enough to claim a place. These are three conditions that ADHD is specifically likely to undermine: awareness without external reminder, time-specific readiness, and rapid action under time pressure. The institutional route that is positioned as the accessible alternative thus selects against the very population it is meant to serve.

### Theme 2 — Unmedicated-by-choice as a distinct and underrepresented user state

Most participants in the corpus are either medicated or awaiting a decision on medication. Theo is the only participant who has decided not to pursue medication (at this stage) due to cost concerns, while being fully diagnosed. This creates a distinct profile: the post-diagnosis period for him is not a medication-titration phase (the typical clinical framing) but a self-management-only period with no clinical scaffolding. His coping strategies are therefore entirely self-generated. This is a user who is in scope (post-diagnosis, first 12 months) but who will not be well-served by a product that assumes medication as a baseline or frames the post-diagnosis journey primarily as medication management.

### Theme 3 — Self-derived strategy as a sign of unmet support, not successful independence

Theo's nine-minutes-on, one-minute-off study regime is sometimes easy to read as evidence that he is coping. A more careful reading reveals it as evidence of a gap: he independently converged on a Pomodoro-like structure through observation and trial and error, with no guidance. The "parameters were just eyeballed" — meaning he iterated until something worked without knowing whether he was starting from the right place, or whether a different parameterisation might work better. A structured support service could have given him a validated starting framework, accelerating the self-calibration process and reducing the period of sub-optimal performance. His success is real; the absence of support that should have shortened his path to it is also real.

### Theme 4 — Body-doubling as the effective mechanism, and the failure of solo-focus tools

The Flown data point is significant not merely as a product-failure anecdote but as a controlled comparison: Theo tried the designated ADHD-focus tool and tried social studying, and social studying won. "Accountability enables more effective studying" is his own causal explanation. Flown is designed specifically for ADHD co-working (virtual body-doubling), which makes its failure here interesting — either Theo's body-doubling need is better served by in-person co-presence than a digital simulation of it, or the app's additional features (structured sessions, interface elements) were the "encumbrance" he identified. Either reading points toward a product design principle: co-presence and accountability are the mechanism; the product layer should be as thin and frictionless as possible around that mechanism, not a rich feature set competing with it.

### Theme 5 — Employment stigma as a distinct and unexplored design constraint

No other participant in the corpus names employment specifically as a stigma vector with the precision Theo does. His framing is strategic, not emotional: he is not describing a fear of being judged — he is describing a rational calculation that disclosure to employers during recruitment is a net negative. This has a direct implication for any platform built around ADHD identity: if the platform requires disclosure (a profile that identifies the user as having ADHD, linked accounts, employer-visible records), users like Theo will either not use it or not use it honestly. Privacy and identity control are not soft UX concerns — they are load-bearing features for this population.

### Theme 6 — Post-diagnosis identity reframing as an unscaffolded emotional process

Theo's account of the post-diagnosis period includes a named emotional arc: sadness at diagnosis, time needed to process that "this didn't change him," followed by the more generative "nice to put a label" framing. This arc was navigated without any structured support — peer community helped with the social side ("made social vibes easier"), but the internal identity work was done alone. A platform that only addresses study strategies and co-working would miss this: newly diagnosed adults need somewhere to process the identity disruption of diagnosis before they are ready to optimise around it.

## 4. Distinctive Contribution

Theo's most distinctive contributions to the DRP corpus are four in number and interrelated.

**First**, the Imperial disability-office access bottleneck — a drop-in that fills in under six minutes — is an observation no other participant provides. It documents how university-route ADHD support replicates NHS-style scarcity at a smaller scale, and does so in a format (timed-release drop-in slots) that is particularly hostile to the ADHD traits of time-awareness and rapid action.

**Second**, Theo is the only fully diagnosed, unmedicated-by-choice participant in the corpus, making him the only proxy for a post-diagnosis user whose entire management strategy is non-pharmacological and self-generated. This user state is within the locked scope but would be systematically underserved by any product that assumes medication as a starting condition.

**Third**, the Flown-to-social-studying switch — combined with the explicit causal statement "accountability enables more effective studying" — provides the strongest single-participant evidence for body-doubling as the effective mechanism and against solo-focus tools as the design direction.

**Fourth**, the employment stigma observation is the most strategically precise stigma data in the corpus: not a vague fear of judgement but a specific calculation that ADHD disclosure in a recruitment context is professionally risky. This constrains platform design toward strong privacy and identity control as non-negotiable features.

## 5. Connection to Locked Problem Scope

**Directly in scope — post-diagnosis, first 12 months, unmedicated by choice.**

Theo is, of all participants in the corpus, the one whose life situation maps most precisely onto the locked scope. He is in the first 12 months post-diagnosis (first-year student, recently diagnosed via the Imperial route). He is unmedicated — not because medication was unavailable, but because cost is a barrier, meaning he is navigating the post-diagnosis period without clinical scaffolding of any kind. The locked problem statement specifies "clinical support is delayed by years and ends abruptly after medication" — for Theo, the clinical support ended before medication even began.

**Uniquely covers the university institutional route.** The other participants diagnosed at university (mentioned briefly in the corpus) navigated similar routes; Theo provides the most detailed account of the Imperial-specific pathway, including its access bottleneck and the external test component. This is directly useful for the "tangible access to diverse stakeholder groups" rubric criterion.

**Does not challenge the scope.** Theo's data does not create pressure to widen or shift the problem framing. His out-of-scope concerns (longer-term career navigation with ADHD, whether to pursue medication in future) are acknowledged but not foregrounded. The acute needs he expresses — study support, identity scaffolding, accountability structures, stigma-safe interaction — all sit squarely within what Option C is designed to address.

## 8. Design Implications

### Implication 1 — Build body-doubling and accountability as core mechanics, not optional features

Theo's switch from Flown to social studying is the clearest participant-level controlled comparison in the corpus: he tested a digital solution and a social solution for the same problem, and the social solution won on its own merits. The stated mechanism is accountability — not gamification, not structured sessions, not AI. The product should centre real-time co-presence (body-doubling rooms, shared focus sessions with visible peers) as a primary interaction mode, with minimal feature overhead around that core. **Failed tool:** Flown — purpose-built ADHD focus tool, abandoned as an "encumbrance."

*Tied to:* "Found it an encumbrance and switched to j studying w other ppl … accountability enables more effective studying."

### Implication 2 — Point away from solo-focus features and tools-with-many-features

Both the Flown failure and the "encumbrance" language point to the same failure mode as the 29yr medicated worker's habit-tracker data: additional features add cognitive load for a brain already managing executive function deficits. A touchpoint that layers timers, AI nudges, session analytics, and focus scores on top of a co-working mechanism will replicate Flown's failure. The product should default to the thinnest possible interface around the accountability mechanism — letting the social layer do the work.

*Tied to:* "Found it an encumbrance."

### Implication 3 — Support post-diagnosis identity-making, not just strategy-building

Theo's data documents a specific emotional arc in the post-diagnosis period: initial sadness, gradual adjustment, and arrival at a generative "technical" reframing of his ADHD. This arc was navigated without structured support. A touchpoint that moves immediately to productivity features skips the identity-processing phase that many newly diagnosed users are actually in. Design should include a layer specifically for newly diagnosed users — structured peer conversations about what diagnosis means, space to develop a personal explanatory model, framing resources that use mechanistic language (for users like Theo) alongside emotional support modes (for users who need those first).

*Tied to:* "Nice to put a label on some of his oddities" and "nice to have a technical train of thought for why he does certain things less efficiently."

### Implication 4 — Privacy and identity control are load-bearing, not UX polish

Theo's employment stigma data is the most precisely articulated in the corpus: he is not wary of social stigma in general, he has performed a specific cost-benefit calculation about professional disclosure and concluded that it is a net negative. For him to use a platform at all, that platform must not require, invite, or risk workplace disclosure. Concretely: no employer-integrated features, no real-name requirements, no visible ADHD labelling in any shareable context, no integration with LinkedIn or professional networks. These are hard constraints, not preferences.

*Tied to:* "Concerned that support from employers would be detrimental to success since only contact w employers is in recruitment context hence wary to supply that information."

### Implication 5 — Design for unmedicated users as a first-class state, not an edge case

The corpus implicitly frames the post-diagnosis journey as converging on medication management. Theo is the clearest counter-example: fully diagnosed, choosing not to medicate (cost barrier), and managing exclusively through self-derived strategies and social support. A product that assumes medication as a baseline — by, for example, framing features around medication timing, or only offering strategies for medicated users — will be less useful or actively irrelevant to this user state. Unmedicated-by-choice is a legitimate, in-scope user state that the product must serve on its own terms.

*Tied to:* "Not tried to get medication yet but doesn't want to get it due to charge" and the self-derived study strategy.
