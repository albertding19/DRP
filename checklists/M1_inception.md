# M1 — Project Inception: Top-Band Checklist
**Elevator Pitch · 3 minutes · Pre-recorded · Due 22 May 2026 · Worth 5%**

> The pitch communicates the **ambition and excitement** of your project idea.
> Assessment is on **content only** — video/production quality is not marked.
> The pitch must cover: problem, target audience, evidence, observations & insights, and a "How Might We?" opportunity statement.

---

## Criterion 1 — Context, Problem and Storytelling

> **Top-band descriptor:** "Rich context, with tangible access to diverse stakeholder groups."

### Actions
- [ ] **Name the place, time, and system** — anchor the problem in a specific, real-world setting (e.g. "a food redistribution charity operating across three London boroughs, Monday–Friday, relying on 40+ volunteers") not a generic domain.
- [ ] **Show tangible access** — explicitly state how and when your team has spoken with or observed people in this context; name the types of people (e.g. "we visited the warehouse and interviewed two project managers and three regular volunteers").
- [ ] **Cover at least two distinct stakeholder groups** — name each group and explain how the problem manifests differently for each (e.g. the coordinator's struggle vs. the volunteer's friction).
- [ ] **Answer all six framing questions in the narration** — who is affected, what is happening, when/where does it occur, why does it matter, how does it play out — even if briefly.
- [ ] **Quantify the scale** using a referenced third-party statistic to establish the wider context (e.g. ONS, academic paper, government report), then immediately ground it with a human story from your own access.
- [ ] **Run a "comfort-zone test"** — confirm the context is not your own team, department, or immediate social circle; if it is, pivot or show evidence of reaching outside it.

### Common pitfalls (lower bands)
- Describing the team's own experience as the primary context ("we as students find X difficult").
- Using family, friends, or colleagues as the only stakeholders without reaching a broader, less familiar group.
- Presenting a scenario with a plausible need but no verified connection to real people in that context.
- Naming only one stakeholder group (e.g. "end users") without exploring who else is involved.

### Evidence/artifact in the pitch
A brief spoken statement or on-screen caption such as: *"We spoke with [Name/Role] at [Organisation/Context] and observed [specific behaviour/friction] on [date]"* — plus a stakeholder map shown on screen for 5–10 seconds identifying at least two groups.

## Criterion 2 — People and Personas

> **Top-band descriptor:** "Well-defined characters/personas used to drive narrative."

### Actions
- [ ] **Create at least two distinct persona cards** before recording — use the RCA template fields: name, age, daily life, knowledge/skills, needs/pains, goals/motivations. Base them on real people you have spoken to, not imagined composites.
- [ ] **Show each persona on screen** during the relevant section of the pitch (a slide or card visible for ≥10 seconds), so the assessor sees the artifact.
- [ ] **Introduce personas early and use them throughout** — let each story beat in the pitch be told from a persona's perspective ("Maria, a project manager, spends 2 hours each Monday morning manually texting volunteers…").
- [ ] **Include direct quotes or paraphrased words** from at least one real participant to give the persona authenticity (e.g. "She told us: 'I never know who's actually going to turn up'").
- [ ] **Distinguish the personas clearly** — different roles, different friction points, different motivations; avoid two personas that are effectively the same person.
- [ ] **End the pitch with a persona callback** — the opportunity statement should explicitly name which personas benefit and how.

### Common pitfalls (lower bands)
- Creating personas with no link to real people ("we imagined a user called Alex who…").
- Producing well-researched personas but then narrating around them rather than through them.
- Presenting only one persona for the entire pitch.
- Personas that are overly abstracted — described only in demographics with no lived-experience detail.

### Evidence/artifact in the pitch
Persona card(s) visible on screen with name, photo/avatar, role, and at least one direct quote or specific behavioural observation attributed to a real conversation.

## Criterion 3 — Evidence

> **Top-band descriptor:** "Strong evidence, providing powerful qualitative arguments, linked with well-referenced third-party statistics."

### Actions
- [ ] **Conduct at least one interactive HCD session before the pitch** — a user interview, observation/field visit, walk-through, or empathy-mapping session (not a survey or WhatsApp message). Aim for 2–4 well-chosen participants across your stakeholder groups.
- [ ] **Reference a credible third-party statistic** that contextualises the scale or severity of the problem — cite the source on-screen (author/org, year). Suitable sources: academic papers, ONS, government reports, industry bodies, commercial research firms.
- [ ] **Pair each statistic with a human data point** — e.g. "the ONS reports X million Y; what that looks like in practice is: [participant quote/observation]."
- [ ] **Show your primary evidence method** — a photograph from a site visit, a screenshot of an interview note, an empathy map, or an AEIOU observation card shown briefly on screen.
- [ ] **Avoid generic facts you haven't linked to your specific context** — every statistic must visibly connect to the problem you are solving.
- [ ] **Document consent** — note in the pitch (or be ready to confirm if asked) that participants agreed to their information being used in your research.

### Common pitfalls (lower bands)
- Relying entirely on desk research (statistics with no first-hand human contact).
- Using a static online survey as the sole primary source — this is explicitly listed as weak evidence.
- Presenting data "as analysed by a third party" without connecting it to any first-hand observation.
- Citing generic facts (e.g. "the global market for X is $Y billion") that don't speak to your specific user's lived reality.
- Hearsay: "we heard that people find X difficult" with no documented source.

### Evidence/artifact in the pitch
On-screen slide or spoken citation in the format: *"[Source, year]: [statistic]"* — plus a visible primary research artifact (photo, empathy map excerpt, interview note snippet) attributable to a real session with real participants.

## Criterion 4 — Insights and Analysis

> **Top-band descriptor:** "Insightful and empathetic observations, with high impact and potential value."

### Actions
- [ ] **Run a synthesis session** after your research (before recording) — cluster your raw observations into themes using an affinity diagram or empathy map; the insight statements that emerge from that session are what you pitch.
- [ ] **Express insights as "we discovered that…" statements** tied to a specific observed behaviour, not a general opinion: e.g. "We discovered that coordinators don't trust digital RSVPs — they phone each volunteer individually because a 'yes' on WhatsApp has historically meant a no-show."
- [ ] **Articulate why this insight is non-obvious** — what did you learn that you could only know by talking to real people? The assessors are listening for the moment of surprise or revelation.
- [ ] **Connect each insight to a consequence** — state the downstream impact (emotional, operational, social) that the unmet need creates for your personas.
- [ ] **Avoid clichés** — do not pitch insights such as "time is money," "people are busy," or "technology makes life easier" without a concrete, specific example that gives the cliché real weight.
- [ ] **Use the empathy map structure** (think/feel, say/do, hear, see, pain, gain) as an internal check: can you populate all quadrants for at least one persona? If a quadrant is empty, your evidence is thin.

### Common pitfalls (lower bands)
- Stating an obvious, generic truth without grounding it in observed behaviour.
- Describing a hypothesis rather than an observation — "we think users probably want X" signals you haven't yet talked to them.
- Specific and authentic insights that are not connected to any clear impact or design opportunity — the "so what?" is missing.
- Reading off a list of bullet-point facts without translating them into meaning.

### Evidence/artifact in the pitch
A spoken insight statement clearly attributed to a research finding (not to team opinion), ideally accompanied by a quotation or a brief visual of an empathy map or affinity cluster on screen.

## Criterion 5 — Opportunity Statement

> **Top-band descriptor:** "Clear 'How Might We' statement covering impact for all stakeholder groups. Ambitious and interesting."

### Actions
- [ ] **Draft the HMW statement using the full formula:** "How Might We [enable/help/empower] [named stakeholder group(s)] to [achieve goal / overcome friction] in order to [broader positive outcome]?" — every part of this formula must be populated.
- [ ] **Name all key stakeholder groups** in the statement or in the sentence immediately following it — the assessors specifically check that it is not written for only one user type.
- [ ] **Test for ambition** — the statement should describe an outcome that is meaningfully better for the people involved, not merely "easier" or "faster." Ask: does this matter? Would a journalist write about it?
- [ ] **Test for feasibility** — confirm the HMW is not so broad it cannot be addressed by a multi-user web or mobile app, and not so narrow it only serves a single edge case.
- [ ] **Read the statement aloud** in the pitch; do not bury it in a slide footnote. Make it the verbal and visual climax of the pitch.
- [ ] **Avoid technology-first framing** — "How Might We build an app that…" is a lower-band statement. The HMW should describe the human need, not the solution mechanism.
- [ ] **Avoid problem-echo statements** — "How Might We reduce the problem of X" simply restates the problem; the statement must articulate a positive opportunity and imply value.

### Common pitfalls (lower bands)
- Missing or vague HMW: "We want to help people with food waste" — no named stakeholder, no mechanism, no outcome.
- Technology-focused: "How Might We design an app to manage volunteers?" — solution-first, not people-first.
- Problem-focused: "How Might We reduce volunteer no-shows?" — valid need, but lacks the positive vision and fails to address other stakeholder groups.
- Incomplete coverage: a strong statement for the primary user but no mention of how secondary stakeholders benefit.

### Evidence/artifact in the pitch
The HMW statement displayed verbatim on screen for the final 20–30 seconds of the pitch, with all stakeholder groups visibly named. If there are two closely related HMW statements (one per major stakeholder group), both should appear.

## Top-Band Summary — Highest-Leverage Moves

These five actions, more than anything else, separate top-band pitches from the rest:

1. **Get in front of real, diverse, external people before Wednesday** — no amount of desk research compensates for the absence of authentic first-hand evidence. Book at least two interviews or one observation session by end of Monday.

2. **Build personas from real conversations, then let them drive every sentence of the pitch** — the narrative should feel like you are telling the story of Maria and James, not describing an abstract user group.

3. **Pair every statistic with a human moment** — a referenced third-party figure earns its place only when it is immediately grounded in something a real participant said or did.

4. **Run a synthesis session and write explicit insight statements before you write the script** — insights that come from the "how we discovered it" process are always more specific, more authentic, and more impactful than insights drafted at a desk.

5. **Write the HMW statement last, display it prominently, and name all stakeholder groups** — the opportunity statement is the payoff of the entire pitch; it must be ambitious, human-centred, and visibly inclusive of everyone affected.
