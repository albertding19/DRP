# Final Demonstration & Presentation — Top Mark Band Checklist

> **Weight:** 50% of total grade | **Format:** 20-min integrated demo + presentation, then 5-min panel Q&A
> **Date:** 16–17 June 2026 | **Final clinic:** 15 June 2026
> **Rule:** ALL members present; each speaks for an equal amount of time.
> **Scoring:** 0 / 1 / 2 / 3 per item — you need **3 on every item** for the top band.

---

## QUALITY OF PRESENTATION

### 1. Good Pacing / Clear Articulation
**Score 3 looks like:** Every speaker delivers at a measured, confident pace with no rushed sections, no dead silence, and no filler words ("um", "uh", "basically"). Transitions between speakers feel natural and rehearsed.

- [ ] Record a full run-through and play it back — mark every moment where pace accelerates or dips; fix those sections.
- [ ] Each speaker prepares a short (2–3 sentence) bridging line to hand off to the next speaker by name.
- [ ] Run the whole presentation at least **three times** with a timer — aim for 18:30–19:30 to land safely inside 20 minutes.
- [ ] Every speaker practises their section alone and receives verbal feedback from teammates on filler words and pace.
- [ ] Draft speaker notes / cue cards for each slide so no one hunts for words mid-slide.

**Common pitfall:** Teams rush the final third (conclusion) because the demo ran long — practise with a hard demo time-cap so the conclusion never gets squeezed.

---

### 2. Clean, Clear and Consistent Slides
**Score 3 looks like:** A single visual theme is applied throughout: consistent fonts, colour palette, spacing, and icon style. Every slide is immediately readable — no walls of text, no contrast issues, no mixed styles from different team members.

- [ ] Agree on a slide template (font, heading size, accent colour, slide margins) before anyone builds content — apply it to every slide.
- [ ] Cap body text at **3–5 bullet points per slide, max 8 words per bullet**; move detail into the spoken word.
- [ ] Export slides to PDF and view on a different screen to catch contrast and font-size issues.
- [ ] Remove or consolidate any slides that duplicate information already shown in the demo.
- [ ] Number every slide and include a subtle progress indicator (section name or slide number) so the panel can orient themselves.

**Common pitfall:** Members build their own slides independently; you end up with four font sizes and three colour palettes — unify everything 48 hours before the presentation.

---

### 3. Ran to Time
**Score 3 looks like:** The presentation starts and finishes within the 20-minute window — the panel never has to stop the team, and no section is clearly cut short.

- [ ] Assign a **hard per-section time budget** and paste it in the speaker notes of the first slide of each section (e.g., Intro 3 min / Problem 4 min / Demo 7 min / Arch + Justification 3 min / Conclusion 3 min).
- [ ] Nominate one person as the silent timekeeper who signals "2 minutes left" and "stop" using a visible cue (raised hand or card).
- [ ] Rehearse cutting the demo to a **strict 7-minute version** — know in advance which features to skip if you are running late.
- [ ] Do at least one full dress rehearsal with a phone timer running and **stop at 20:00 no matter what**.
- [ ] Build in a deliberate 30-second buffer per major transition so small overruns do not cascade.

**Common pitfall:** Live demos are the #1 time sink — teams underestimate loading time, login time, and explanatory commentary; time the demo segment separately.

---

### 4. Sensible Use of Diagrams/Images
**Score 3 looks like:** Every diagram and image earns its place — it communicates something that words alone cannot. Diagrams are large enough to read from the back of the room, correctly labelled, and referred to explicitly by the presenter.

- [ ] For each diagram in the deck, the presenting team member must say what it shows and what conclusion to draw — if they cannot, remove the diagram.
- [ ] Check every image/diagram at **1920×1080 full screen** — any text smaller than ~20 pt on-screen should be cut or enlarged.
- [ ] Use at least one **before/after user journey map** to anchor the problem and solution visually.
- [ ] Use your system architecture diagram (see Technical Quality section) as the central visual during the architecture section — animate it to highlight the component being discussed.
- [ ] Avoid stock photos and clip-art; prefer screenshots from your own app, photos from your own user-testing sessions, and diagrams you drew.

**Common pitfall:** Diagram-heavy slides where the presenter reads callouts line by line — panels find this condescending. Point, explain briefly, move on.

---

### 5. Emotive Presentation
**Score 3 looks like:** The team conveys genuine conviction that their problem matters and their solution works. The panel feels the user's pain and sees why the team cares. At least one concrete user story creates emotional resonance.

- [ ] Open with a **30–60 second real-user vignette** (a quote, a photo, a short story from your field research) that makes the problem tangible before you show any technology.
- [ ] Each member speaks about the part of the project they personally drove — passion reads as authentic; rehearsed enthusiasm does not.
- [ ] Include at least **one direct quote** from a real user (with first name, brief descriptor) on a slide or spoken aloud.
- [ ] End with a **"what this means for [user name]" sentence** that ties the conclusion back to the opening vignette.
- [ ] Practise making eye contact with the panel, not the screen — use speaker view or notes cards so you do not need to turn around.

**Common pitfall:** Teams default to a "here is what we built" technical report tone — the panel wants to be persuaded, not briefed. Treat it as a pitch, not a lecture.

---

### 6. Demo Integrated with Presentation
**Score 3 looks like:** The live demo is not a separate "and now we'll show you the app" block — it is woven into the narrative. Features are demonstrated at the moment they become relevant to the story being told.

- [ ] Map every demo step to a slide or spoken claim — the demo proves what you just said, not something unrelated.
- [ ] Practise the demo inside the slide deck (e.g., use a browser window docked next to slides in presenter mode, or a split-screen layout).
- [ ] Start and end the demo with a **clear verbal signal** ("watch what happens when a user does X..." / "so that confirms what we said about Y").
- [ ] Avoid switching contexts mid-demo (no alt-tabbing to unrelated windows, no accidental notifications visible on screen).
- [ ] The person driving the demo must speak continuously — silence during loading screens or navigation is dead airtime; prepare filler lines.

**Common pitfall:** Demo is bolted on as a 7-minute "now you drive the app" block with no narration — the panel sees features but does not understand why they matter.

---

## PRESENTATION CONTENT — STORY TELLING

### 7. Quantitative Evidence / Background Research
**Score 3 looks like:** Hard statistics (with sources) are woven naturally into the narrative to establish the scale and urgency of the problem. Numbers are precise, sourced, and visually highlighted.

- [ ] Include **at least 3 cited statistics** from reputable sources (government datasets, peer-reviewed research, established charities/NGOs) that quantify the problem.
- [ ] Display each stat as a **"hero number"** on its own visual element rather than buried in a bullet — e.g., a large-font callout card.
- [ ] Cross-reference your own quantitative evaluation data (iteration 4 metrics) against baseline or benchmark — show movement.
- [ ] Cite sources in small text on the slide (author, year, URL) and keep a references slide at the back for the Q&A.
- [ ] Make sure every number you quote, any team member can explain and defend in Q&A — no cherry-picked stats you cannot contextualise.

**Common pitfall:** Statistics appear only in the problem statement section and then disappear — weave them back into the conclusion ("our solution reduced X by Y%").

---

### 8. Qualitative Evidence / Real User Stories
**Score 3 looks like:** Verbatim quotes, field observations, or short narratives from real, named (or appropriately anonymised) users appear throughout the deck and are explicitly linked to design decisions.

- [ ] Include **at least 2 direct user quotes** (verbatim, with context: who said it, when, during which iteration).
- [ ] Show at least one **persona card** or user profile on screen to ground the story in a real individual, not "our users".
- [ ] For each major design decision (feature added, feature cut, UI change), name the user-testing finding that drove it.
- [ ] If you filmed any user-testing sessions, extract a **10–15 second clip** for the demo — even one clip dramatically increases authenticity.
- [ ] Present qualitative data with appropriate nuance — acknowledge contrasting opinions from different user groups rather than presenting a monolithic "users said X".

**Common pitfall:** Qualitative evidence is listed as "we interviewed 5 users and they liked it" — the panel needs the actual content of what users said and how it changed your design.

## THE PROBLEM

### 9. Clear Problem Statement
**Score 3 looks like:** A crisp, single-sentence problem statement that any intelligent non-expert can understand. It is displayed prominently early in the presentation and referenced again in the conclusion.

- [ ] Write the problem statement as **one sentence, ≤ 25 words**, in plain English (no jargon) — display it on its own slide.
- [ ] Frame it as a **user need**, not a technology gap: "X people struggle to do Y because Z" not "there is no app for X".
- [ ] Revisit the problem statement verbatim in the conclusion slide to show how your solution addresses it.
- [ ] Agree as a team on the exact wording — every member should be able to recite it from memory.
- [ ] Test it on someone outside the project; if they need clarification, the statement is not clear enough.

**Common pitfall:** The problem statement shifts subtly between speakers — by the conclusion, the problem being "solved" is different from the one stated in the intro.

---

### 10. Real-World Evidence-Driven Problem
**Score 3 looks like:** The problem is grounded in field research, not assumption. Evidence includes first-hand observation, interviews with real stakeholders, and corroborating third-party data — all conducted before committing to a solution.

- [ ] Show at least one **field research artefact** on screen: an AEIOU observation note, a photo from a site visit, or an affinity diagram.
- [ ] Name the specific research methods used (AEIOU, stakeholder interviews, contextual inquiry) and when they were carried out (iteration 1 or 2).
- [ ] Reference at least one finding that **surprised the team or changed direction** — this demonstrates genuine discovery, not confirmation of prior assumptions.
- [ ] Connect each claim about the problem to a specific evidence source, not "we found that users…" but "during iteration 1, participant [A], a [descriptor], told us…".
- [ ] Acknowledge the **scope of your research sample** honestly — and explain how you managed for diversity.

**Common pitfall:** "Research" turns out to be a single survey sent to friends — panels distinguish between convenience sampling and real field research immediately.

---

### 11. Well-Defined Target Audience
**Score 3 looks like:** The target audience is described with enough specificity that you could identify a real individual who fits it. Multiple distinct stakeholder groups are named, and the primary user is clearly prioritised.

- [ ] Display a **stakeholder map** showing primary users, secondary stakeholders, and their relationships.
- [ ] Present at least **one persona** with: name, age/role, goals, frustrations, and a quote — driven by research, not invented.
- [ ] Explicitly state *why this group* and *why now* — the audience must feel the choice was deliberate.
- [ ] Show how different stakeholder groups have different needs and how your design balances them.
- [ ] Confirm that your target audience includes people **outside your team and social circle**.

**Common pitfall:** Target audience described as "anyone who…" or "young people" — vague audiences signal that no real user research was done.

---

### 12. Distinct User Testing Iterations
**Score 3 looks like:** Four visibly separate rounds of user testing are narrated with clear before/after evidence: what you tested, who you tested with, what you found, and what changed as a result.

- [ ] Create a **two-column "Before / After" slide** or table for each iteration showing: design decision before testing vs. design decision after testing.
- [ ] Name a specific participant (or participant type) for each iteration's testing — "three commuters aged 25–40" is better than "some users".
- [ ] Describe at least one finding from each iteration that directly caused a **concrete change** to the product.
- [ ] Show the evolution of a key screen across all four iterations — a visual timeline of change is powerful and fast to read.
- [ ] Distinguish between usability testing, prototype testing, and in-context testing — demonstrate you used multiple methods.

**Common pitfall:** Iterations blend together ("we kept testing throughout") — the panel needs to see four distinct, documented cycles, each with a clear loop: build → test → learn → change.

## USER EXPERIENCE

### 13. Relevant Stakeholder Groups Considered
**Score 3 looks like:** The design explicitly accounts for the needs of multiple distinct groups (e.g., end user, administrator, bystander, service provider) — and trade-offs between their needs are acknowledged.

- [ ] Name every stakeholder group that was included in research or design decisions, and show them on the stakeholder map.
- [ ] For each major feature, state which stakeholder group it primarily serves and which secondary groups benefit.
- [ ] Show at least one design decision that required **balancing competing stakeholder needs** — and explain how you resolved it.
- [ ] Confirm that marginalised or less-visible stakeholders (e.g., people with lower digital literacy, users with accessibility needs) were considered and, where possible, included in testing.
- [ ] Reference back to stakeholder groups in the conclusion when discussing impact and future work.

**Common pitfall:** Only the primary user is discussed — the panel expects to see that the team mapped and considered the full ecosystem of people affected.

---

### 14. Seamless In-App User Experience
**Score 3 looks like:** The demo shows a fluid, error-free flow through the core user journey. Navigation is obvious, actions produce immediate feedback, and the UI communicates state clearly.

- [ ] Run through the demo flow **10+ times** on the exact machine/browser/account you will use on the day — log every glitch and fix it.
- [ ] Ensure all core user journeys work with **pre-seeded, realistic data** (no empty lists, no placeholder text like "Lorem ipsum").
- [ ] Test with realistic network conditions (not localhost) — if deploying on CloudStack, demo from CloudStack.
- [ ] Every interactive element must provide **immediate visual feedback** (loading states, success/error messages) — verify these all work.
- [ ] Conduct a usability walk-through with someone who has never seen the app — fix anything that confuses them.

**Common pitfall:** Demo crashes or shows an error during the 20-minute window — this single event can tank Technical Quality scores across multiple items.

---

### 15. Seamless In-Context User Experience
**Score 3 looks like:** The presentation makes clear how the digital touchpoint fits within the broader real-world context of use — it is not described as an isolated app but as one step in a wider service or experience.

- [ ] Show a **user journey map** (before and after your touchpoint) that situates the app within the wider context — what happens before and after someone uses it.
- [ ] Describe the **physical or social environment** where users access the touchpoint — show photos or sketches from your research.
- [ ] Demonstrate or describe how the app handles real-world constraints (poor connectivity, interrupted sessions, shared device use).
- [ ] If your touchpoint is part of a service blueprint, show the blueprint and point out where the app sits.
- [ ] Address how users discover and onboard into the app in context — not just what they do inside it.

**Common pitfall:** Teams demonstrate the app as if users sit at a desktop with full attention — ignoring the real environment (on the move, distracted, limited data) loses marks here.

---

### 16. Navigable UI
**Score 3 looks like:** Any new user watching the demo can understand how to move through the app without explanation. Navigation is consistent, labelled, and forgiving (easy to go back, undo, recover from errors).

- [ ] Include a **screen-flow diagram** or app map in the presentation showing every screen and how they connect.
- [ ] During the demo, narrate navigation explicitly: "I'm tapping the menu here — it takes me to..." so the panel can follow.
- [ ] Test navigation with **at least two people who have never used the app** and fix any routes they found confusing.
- [ ] Verify that every screen has a clear way back — no dead ends.
- [ ] Confirm that navigation works consistently across the device sizes / browsers you will demo on.

**Common pitfall:** The demo operator navigates quickly from muscle memory — the panel cannot follow the UI structure at speed; slow down and narrate every step.

---

## TECHNICAL QUALITY

### 17. Justified Language/Framework Choice
**Score 3 looks like:** The presentation explicitly compares the chosen stack against at least two alternatives on concrete technical criteria (performance, real-time capability, team familiarity, deployment constraints) and explains why the chosen stack won.

- [ ] Prepare a **comparison table** (chosen tech vs. 2 alternatives) covering: real-time support, database integration, learning curve, deployment compatibility, community support.
- [ ] State which criteria mattered most **for your specific problem** — a generic comparison scores lower than a problem-specific one.
- [ ] Acknowledge genuine trade-offs — "we chose X but Y would have been better for Z" is honest and impressive.
- [ ] Every team member should be able to name the framework and give one-sentence justifications — this comes up in Q&A.
- [ ] Tie the justification back to technical requirements: real-time interactions, CI/CD, database, multi-user — show the chosen stack satisfies them.

**Common pitfall:** Justification amounts to "we already knew it" — panels want technical reasoning, not comfort justification.

---

### 18. User-to-User Interactions
**Score 3 looks like:** The demo shows real-time interaction between two or more simultaneous users — changes made by one user are visibly reflected for another, live, during the demo.

- [ ] **Demonstrate with two physical devices / browsers** open simultaneously — show one user's action updating the other's view in real time.
- [ ] Narrate the multi-user moment explicitly: "notice that when [user A] does this, [user B]'s screen updates immediately".
- [ ] Pre-log in two test accounts before the presentation begins — do not waste demo time on authentication.
- [ ] Test the real-time interaction on the deployment environment (not localhost) at least once before the day.
- [ ] If real-time breaks, have a **screenshot or screen-recording fallback** ready to show as evidence.

**Common pitfall:** Teams "explain" real-time features without showing them live — the spec requires demonstration of technical workings; description alone scores 1, not 3.

---

### 19. Effective System Architecture Diagram
**Score 3 looks like:** A single, clean diagram shows every major component (client, server, database, real-time layer, CI/CD pipeline, external APIs) and the data/control flows between them, at the right level of abstraction.

- [ ] Draw the architecture diagram from scratch for this presentation — do not reuse a rough sketch from an earlier milestone.
- [ ] Label every component and every arrow — what flows along each connection (HTTP request, WebSocket event, SQL query, etc.).
- [ ] Animate the diagram during the presentation to highlight the component being discussed at that moment.
- [ ] Include the **CI/CD pipeline** as a component — show how code goes from commit to deployment.
- [ ] Test: can someone who has not seen the app understand how data flows from user action to database and back in under 60 seconds? If not, simplify.

**Common pitfall:** Architecture diagram shows components but not interactions — a list of boxes with no arrows is not an architecture diagram, it is an inventory.

---

### 20. Responsive User Interactions
**Score 3 looks like:** Every user action in the demo produces a visible response within an acceptable time — no perceptible lag, no hanging spinners, no unacknowledged button presses.

- [ ] **Load-test the app** before the presentation: simulate 3–5 concurrent users on the deployment environment and verify response times stay acceptable.
- [ ] Add loading indicators or optimistic UI updates for every action that involves a network call — so the app never appears frozen.
- [ ] Pre-cache or pre-load any assets (images, maps, external data) that might cause slow initial loads.
- [ ] During the demo, avoid triggering any heavy background jobs that could degrade responsiveness.
- [ ] Rehearse the demo on the actual lab machine or venue laptop — your dev machine may be significantly faster than the presentation environment.

**Common pitfall:** The demo environment is slower than the dev environment; teams discover this on the day and cannot fix it — test on the real machine at least 24 hours before.

---

### 21. Stable Product
**Score 3 looks like:** The app runs without crashes, errors, or unexpected behaviour throughout the entire 20-minute window. Edge cases and unexpected inputs are handled gracefully.

- [ ] Freeze the codebase at least **48 hours before** the presentation — no new features, only bug fixes.
- [ ] Write a **pre-demo smoke-test checklist** (login, core journey, real-time feature, database write/read) and run it immediately before going on stage.
- [ ] Test with **boundary/invalid inputs** (empty fields, special characters, rapid repeated actions) — confirm the app handles them without crashing.
- [ ] Ensure the app tolerates the panel clicking or tapping randomly — assume the panel will interact; lock down or hide anything unfinished.
- [ ] Have a **tagged, working release commit** ready so you can roll back instantly if a last-minute change breaks something.

**Common pitfall:** A "just one more feature" commit the night before the presentation introduces a regression that breaks the demo — enforce the feature freeze ruthlessly.

## CONCLUSION AND EVALUATION

### 22. What Went Well?
**Score 3 looks like:** Specific, evidence-backed claims about successes — not "we worked well as a team" but "our real-time sync achieved < 200ms latency, confirmed by user-testing feedback in iteration 4".

- [ ] Identify **3–4 concrete successes**, each backed by a metric or user feedback quote.
- [ ] Link at least one "went well" point to a decision made in response to user testing — shows HCD feedback loop worked.
- [ ] Keep this section brief (60–90 seconds) — it is context for the more important "differently" and "future work" sections.
- [ ] Avoid generic team-process praise unless you can quantify it (e.g., "we merged 47 PRs across 4 iterations with zero broken deployments").
- [ ] Ensure every team member's contribution is visible somewhere in the "went well" narrative.

**Common pitfall:** "What went well" fills the whole conclusion, leaving 20 seconds for "differently" and "future work" — distribute time deliberately.

---

### 23. User Impact / Benefits
**Score 3 looks like:** The presentation quantifies or qualitatively evidences the positive change your touchpoint creates for real users — not theoretical benefits but measured or observed ones.

- [ ] Report at least **one quantitative metric** measuring user impact (task completion rate, time saved, satisfaction score, frequency of use) gathered during iteration 4 evaluation.
- [ ] Contrast this metric against a **baseline or benchmark** — "before our app, users took 12 minutes; with it, 3 minutes".
- [ ] Include a **user quote about impact** — "this would genuinely change how I…" — from your final-iteration testing.
- [ ] Discuss impact on all relevant stakeholder groups, not just the primary user.
- [ ] If you created an "impact asset" (iteration 4 requirement), reference or show it.

**Common pitfall:** Benefits described are hypothetical ("users would benefit from…") rather than evidenced — panels want to see that you tested impact, not imagined it.

---

### 24. What Would You Do Differently?
**Score 3 looks like:** Honest, specific, technically grounded reflections — not superficial ("we would start earlier") but substantive ("we would have chosen WebSockets over polling from the outset, because latency in iterations 1–2 cost us three user-testing sessions").

- [ ] Prepare **3 specific "differently" points** — at least one technical, one HCD/process, one team/organisation.
- [ ] For each, state the **consequence** of the original decision and what the alternative would have changed.
- [ ] Avoid overly negative self-critique — frame each point as a learning, not a failure.
- [ ] Ensure the team has agreed on these points in advance — contradictions between members in Q&A lose marks.
- [ ] Do not include anything that suggests a fundamental flaw in the project's viability — focus on execution, not concept.

**Common pitfall:** "Differently" is vague platitudes ("better time management") — panels see through generic reflection; they reward specific, technically informed critique.

---

### 25. Future Work
**Score 3 looks like:** A prioritised, realistic roadmap of genuinely valuable next steps, directly motivated by user-testing findings or technical limitations discovered during the project.

- [ ] List **3–5 future features or improvements**, each linked to a specific user need or technical finding (not wishful thinking).
- [ ] Prioritise them — which would you tackle first and why? Show you have thought about value vs. effort.
- [ ] Mention at least one **scalability or deployment consideration** (e.g., handling 10× the users, accessibility improvements, multi-language support).
- [ ] Reference any **user requests that were out of scope** for this project — shows your research went deep enough to surface real demand.
- [ ] Keep future work grounded: one or two ambitious visions are fine, but the list should be mostly achievable, not a fantasy roadmap.

**Common pitfall:** Future work is listed as features with no motivation — "we would add X, Y, Z" without explaining *why* scores 1–2, not 3. Each item needs a user or technical reason.

## LIVE DEMO DE-RISKING

- [ ] **Freeze the demo branch** 48 hours before the presentation — deploy from this frozen tag, not from `main`.
- [ ] **Seed realistic data** in the production database before the presentation: real-looking usernames, meaningful content, populated feeds/boards — no empty states or placeholder text.
- [ ] **Record a full-fidelity screen-capture fallback** of the complete demo flow (with audio narration) and have it ready to play from a local file — not a streaming link.
- [ ] **Test on the lab machines / venue hardware** at least 24 hours before: browser version, screen resolution, display scaling, and font rendering can all differ from your dev machine.
- [ ] **Pre-login all accounts** you need for the demo before going on stage — have credentials written on a card in your pocket, not just in a password manager.
- [ ] **Test on the deployment environment** (e.g., DoC CloudStack URL), not localhost — confirm the app is accessible from the lab network.
- [ ] **Simulate network failure**: know what your app shows if the WebSocket drops or a fetch times out — graceful degradation should be visible, not a white crash screen.
- [ ] **Multi-user demo**: bring two physical devices (or two browsers logged in as different users) to demonstrate real-time interaction live — assign who operates each device before the day.
- [ ] **Disable notifications and popups** on all demo devices (do not disturb mode, no Slack/email toasts).
- [ ] **Check audio/video output** in the presentation room in advance if any multimedia is used (video clips, sound).
- [ ] **Have the fallback recording on a USB drive** and emailed to yourself — multiple access paths.
- [ ] **Assign a dedicated demo driver** — the person who knows the app best drives; everyone else narrates. Do not switch drivers mid-demo.

## REHEARSAL & LOGISTICS

- [ ] **Measure speaking time per person** across a full rehearsal — each member must speak for approximately the same duration (within 60–90 seconds of each other).
- [ ] **Assign sections to owners** explicitly: who speaks for intro, problem, demo narration, architecture, framework justification, conclusion — written down and agreed.
- [ ] **Practise slide handovers**: each speaker ends their section with a verbal handoff ("I'll now pass to [name] who will walk us through the architecture") and the next speaker begins immediately.
- [ ] **Run a full timed dress rehearsal** with someone acting as panel: give them permission to interrupt with a question mid-way through to simulate Q&A pressure.
- [ ] **Prepare Q&A answers** for the most likely panel questions:
  - Why did you choose [framework] over [alternative]?
  - How did your design change between iterations 1 and 4?
  - What would a real deployment of this look like at scale?
  - Which user feedback had the biggest impact on your design?
  - Why did you not implement [obvious feature]?
- [ ] **Agree on who handles unscripted panel questions** — designate one "fielder" per topic area rather than everyone talking over each other.
- [ ] **Practise the 20-minute version** at least three times; practise a **17-minute cut-down version** once (in case you need to accelerate).
- [ ] **Confirm who drives the laptop and who handles the demo devices** — this is a role decision, not to be made on the day.

## TOP-BAND SUMMARY — The 5 Highest-Leverage Moves

These actions have the highest return across multiple scored items simultaneously. If time is short, prioritise these.

1. **Run the live demo on the actual deployment environment, with two devices, seeded with real data, at least twice before the day.** This single action de-risks Technical Quality (items 18, 20, 21), UX (items 14, 16), and demo integration (item 6) simultaneously. A single demo crash can pull multiple item scores from 3 to 1.

2. **Build and rehearse your user story arc end-to-end.** A coherent narrative — open with a real user's pain, show research evidence, demonstrate how the touchpoint solves it, close by returning to that user — lifts scores across Story Telling (items 7, 8), The Problem (items 9, 10, 11), Emotive Presentation (item 5), and Conclusion (items 22, 23). This is the single biggest differentiator between a 2 and a 3 on content items.

3. **Enforce the 48-hour feature freeze and run a full timed dress rehearsal.** This directly secures Ran to Time (item 3), Stable Product (item 21), and Good Pacing (item 1). Teams that deploy new code the night before almost always demo something broken. Teams that have not run to time always overrun.

4. **Prepare a justified, comparison-table-backed framework/language slide, and ensure every member can defend the choice in Q&A.** Framework justification (item 17) is the most purely technical scored item and the most common source of Q&A questions. A two-minute, well-reasoned comparison slide also signals technical maturity to the panel across the whole presentation.

5. **Create a visually animated system architecture diagram and practise talking through it in under 90 seconds.** The architecture diagram (item 19) is the centrepiece of your technical section. A diagram that the presenter cannot fluently explain is worse than a simpler one they can — practise until the walkthrough is effortless. This also directly supports Technical Quality credibility across items 17–21.
